## Three ideas

### 1. Ledger
**Pitch:** The back office a creator business never had — contracts, invoicing, tax withholding, all agentic, all cross-platform.

**The problem:** Full-time creators are unregistered small businesses running six-figure revenue through DMs, spreadsheets, and memory. Brand deal terms live in email threads, payment timing is inconsistent across platforms and currencies, and tax withholding is a September surprise. That 20+ hrs/week of unpaid admin (per the Reddit data above) is a second, unpaid job stacked on top of the actual content work.

**Why now:** Instinct just proved the market will hand deep app/device permissions to a general-purpose agent at a $2.5B valuation — the trust threshold for "let an AI act inside my financial life" has visibly moved this year. A vertical version of that, scoped tightly to creator commerce (read brand-deal emails, reconcile payouts from YouTube/TikTok/Patreon/Shopify, auto-file quarterly tax estimates), is a much easier sell than a general life-agent, and doesn't carry Instinct's permission-scope backlash because the scope is narrow and named.

**Mechanism:** Connects read-only to a creator's inbox, bank, and platform payout dashboards. An LLM agent extracts deal terms and payment schedules from emails and contracts, flags underpayment or late payment automatically, and routes a percentage into a tax-holding sub-account on a rolling basis. A human accountant reviews and signs off before anything filing-related goes out — the agent drafts, a licensed pro approves.

**First wedge:** Mid-tier creators ($50K–$500K/yr) with 3+ brand deals a quarter — too big for spreadsheets, too small to hire a manager. Reach them through the same creator-ops communities (Passionfroot's marketplace, creator Discords, accountant-to-the-stars referral networks) already selling adjacent tools.

**What kills it:** Trust. Creators who've been burned by platforms are wary of handing a startup read access to their bank and inbox — the exact backlash Instinct is facing right now. Needs radical transparency (local-first options, clear data deletion, maybe open-sourcing the extraction logic) to clear that bar.

**Weirder variant:** Same engine, aimed at Twitch/OnlyFans-adjacent creators who have the least access to traditional accountants or banks willing to serve them — the underserved end of the market is also the most acute pain.

### 2. Provenance
**Pitch:** A verified "this was actually made this way" receipt for D2C products, issued at the point of manufacture, not marketing.

**The problem:** AI-generated ads, synthetic influencer endorsements, and fabricated "behind the scenes" factory content are now cheap enough to flood every feed. Consumers buying from newer D2C brands (Atorie's whole pitch is cutting out markup by being closer to the actual manufacturer) have no way to tell a real supply-chain claim from a generated one. Brands with genuinely good manufacturing stories are getting drowned out by brands that just generate a better story.

**Why now:** The HN "AI slop" backlash isn't abstract anymore — it's the year editorial judgment/verification stopped being a nice-to-have and started being what people will pay a premium for. At the same time, cheap on-device video and IoT sensors make it feasible to actually capture verifiable factory/process footage at scale, not just claim it.

**Mechanism:** Manufacturers install a lightweight capture kit (camera + sensor package) at the production line. Footage and material-sourcing data get timestamped and hashed at capture time, before any editing touches them, and a compressed "provenance receipt" (a short verified clip plus a data summary) attaches to the product listing and ships with the order. Brands pay per-SKU to onboard; the verification layer is the moat, not the video editing.

**First wedge:** Small-batch, direct-from-factory D2C brands (the exact profile Atorie and its funders are betting on) who already differentiate on "no markup, real factory" — they have the actual story, they just can't prove it's not AI-generated. Reach them through the same DTC accelerators and Shopify app ecosystem.

**What kills it:** Adoption chicken-and-egg — a receipt only matters once shoppers recognize the badge, and shoppers only learn the badge once enough brands carry it. Needs a retailer or platform (Shopify, a marketplace) to bundle it in by default rather than selling brand-by-brand.

**Weirder variant:** Turn it inward — a provenance layer for creator content itself (a "this clip wasn't generated" receipt attached at the camera), sold to news orgs and documentary creators instead of D2C brands.

---

### 3. Slipstream
**Pitch:** Parametric insurance for creators — get paid when a platform algorithm change tanks your reach, no claims process.

**The problem:** 58% of creators report real anxiety about algorithm changes, and for good reason — a single update can cut reach in half overnight with zero warning and zero recourse. Unlike almost every other small business, a creator's core revenue driver (distribution) is owned entirely by a third party who owes them nothing and changes the rules unannounced. TikTok's "Creator Sabbatical" and YouTube's counseling line are wellness patches on a business-risk problem.

**Why now:** Platforms now expose enough granular API telemetry (impressions, reach, RPM by day) for a third party to build a reliable, tamper-resistant baseline of a creator's normal performance — the data plumbing for a "reach dropped X% vs. your trailing baseline" trigger didn't reliably exist a couple of years ago. And embedded-finance infrastructure for the creator economy (the same rails Passionfroot and others are building for payments) makes underwriting and payout logistics for a niche product like this actually cheap to run.

**Mechanism:** Creators connect platform analytics (read-only). The product computes a rolling baseline of reach/RPM and sells a monthly parametric policy: if reach drops more than an agreed threshold below baseline for two consecutive weeks with no matching drop in posting volume or content mix, it pays out automatically — no claims adjuster, just a formula. Priced like weather derivatives: pooled risk across thousands of creators smooths the payout cost, since not everyone gets hit by the same algorithm change at once.

**First wedge:** Mid-tier creators on a single dominant platform (highest concentration risk, least diversified income) — reach them through creator manager networks and MCN-style agencies who already sell "protect your income" as a pitch.

**What kills it:** Moral hazard and gaming — a creator could intentionally tank output right before renewal to trigger a payout, or platforms could argue the "no content mix change" clause endlessly. Needs airtight, boring underwriting more than clever growth hacking.

**Weirder variant:** Sell the inverse product to platforms themselves — a hedge that pays creators automatically whenever the platform ships an algorithm change, funded by the platform, as a retention tool instead of an adversarial insurance product.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Slipstream is the weakest of the three.** Parametric insurance only works when the trigger event is unambiguous and hard to game — weather is clean, "algorithm-driven reach drop" is much messier, because reach drops for a dozen mundane reasons (worse thumbnails, seasonal audience shifts, a creator just posting less) and disentangling "the platform changed something" from "the creator's content got worse" is a genuinely hard actuarial problem, not just a data-access problem. It may need a platform's cooperation (confirming "yes, we shipped a ranking change on this date") to be underwritable at all, which no platform has an incentive to give. Worth stress-testing with an actual actuary or someone who's built parametric products before assuming the data access alone makes it viable.

Ledger's trust problem is real but solvable with transparency; Provenance's chicken-and-egg problem is real but solvable with one retailer partnership. Slipstream's core mechanism might just not be underwritable as described.
