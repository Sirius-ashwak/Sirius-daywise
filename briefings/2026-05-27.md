## 3 Startup Ideas

### 1. QueueDesk
**One-line pitch:** The Bloomberg Terminal for power grid interconnection queue positions.

**The specific problem:** Over 2,060 GW of energy projects are sitting in interconnection queues, often for 5–10 years. Developers abandon projects constantly — but their queue positions (which represent years of waiting and real optionality) just evaporate. Meanwhile, developers at the front of the queue are selling grid access informally, on phone calls, with no pricing data and no legal infrastructure. Data center developers are literally buying decommissioned industrial land just to inherit legacy grid connections. There is no marketplace, no price discovery, no due diligence tooling.

**Why now:** FERC Order 1920 (transmission planning reform) is reshaping which queue positions survive. State-level interconnection reforms in PJM, CAISO, and ERCOT are creating new volatility. ThinkLabs just got $28M to speed up studies — that acceleration means more queue positions will get resolved faster, and the secondary market will heat up. This is the exact moment before liquidity forms.

**Mechanism:** QueueDesk is a data and transactions platform. It scrapes and normalizes all public queue data across ISOs (interconnection study statuses, upgrade cost estimates, withdrawal rates). Subscribers get a dashboard showing queue position value estimates based on location, technology, study phase, and comparable transactions. The premium layer is a brokered marketplace where developers can list positions with anonymized deal terms, and buyers can run due diligence through the platform.

**First wedge:** Independent Power Producers (IPPs) with solar or wind projects that have stalled or been abandoned. They have positions with value they can't realize. Reach them through renewable energy attorneys and project finance advisors who already work the informal market — offer to formalize what they're already doing by phone.

**What kills it:** ISOs themselves could build this (FERC has nudged them toward it). Or a large energy data vendor like Enverus or Wood Mackenzie could add a transactions layer. The window is real but may be 18 months wide.

**Weirder variant:** AI data center developers are the most desperate queue position buyers right now. Build QueueDesk exclusively as a data center power acquisition tool, not an energy developer tool. Completely different sales motion, higher willingness to pay, faster deal velocity.

### 2. HeatOS
**One-line pitch:** Operational software that electrifies industrial heat without stopping production.

**The specific problem:** Industrial process heat below 200°C (drying, sterilization, pasteurization, cleaning, curing) accounts for roughly 30% of all industrial energy and is now technically and economically electrifiable with heat pumps and electric boilers. But switching is terrifying for plant operators — they have zero visibility into when their heat loads can safely switch sources, what the load sequencing looks like, and how to do it without triggering a production shutdown. The CapEx decision is getting made; the operational transition is not. Procurement buys a heat pump. Operations has no idea how to run it alongside existing systems.

**Why now:** Industrial heat pump costs dropped ~40% between 2023–2025. IRA manufacturing credits apply to qualifying equipment. The EU's Industrial Decarbonization Accelerator Act (effective Jan 2026) mandates scope 1 emission reduction plans for mid-size manufacturers. Suddenly, the signed contracts exist — but the software to operate through the transition does not.

**Mechanism:** HeatOS connects to a facility's existing SCADA/BMS systems via standard protocols (Modbus, OPC-UA). It builds a digital twin of the site's thermal flows — mapping heat sources, loads, temperatures, timing constraints, and process dependencies. It then generates an electrification transition playbook: a step-by-step operating schedule that sequences the switch load-by-load, identifying low-risk windows (nights, weekends, non-peak production) for each phase. Ongoing, it runs as an optimization layer — dynamically shifting heat pump operation to low-tariff hours and demand response windows, monetizing the installed fleet.

**First wedge:** Food and beverage manufacturers in the EU. They face the stiffest regulatory pressure, their heat loads are relatively predictable (known production schedules), and they have strong sustainability teams who can champion internal. Reach them through the heat pump OEMs (Alfa Laval, Klingenburg, Mayekawa) who sell the hardware but have no software story for the operational transition — classic channel play.

**What kills it:** Industrial SCADA integrations are notoriously slow and expensive. If average integration time exceeds 6 months, CAC kills unit economics before you reach scale. Also: large automation vendors (Siemens, Honeywell) could build this, but their sales cycles are 2 years so you'd still have time.

**Weirder variant:** Skip the manufacturer entirely and sell HeatOS to energy retailers as a white-label demand response product. The retailer offers it free to industrial customers in exchange for controlling their heat load during grid stress events — retailer pays QueueDesk per MWh of flexibility dispatched.

### 3. CreditTrace
**One-line pitch:** Carbon credit forensics for corporate procurement teams under SEC and EU audit scrutiny.

**The specific problem:** Corporate sustainability teams are buying voluntary carbon credits to meet net-zero commitments, but the quality is wildly inconsistent and the registry system is opaque. SEC climate disclosure rules (in effect 2026) and EU CSRD require companies to substantiate emissions claims. Procurement teams are getting burned — internal auditors and external regulators are asking "show me the chain of custody for this credit" and the answer is usually a PDF from a broker and a registry ID. At least three major companies faced SEC comment letters in Q1 2026 over unverifiable offset claims. The team buying credits has no technical way to verify what they're buying before they're on the hook for it.

**Why now:** SEC climate disclosure rules fully in effect for large accelerated filers as of fiscal year 2026. EU CSRD third-party assurance requirements kick in for wave 2 companies. EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is live and creating spillover demand for supply chain carbon accounting rigor. Voluntary carbon market registries (Verra, Gold Standard) are under fire and adding verification requirements — but they're slow and self-serve. The liability just got real.

**Mechanism:** CreditTrace is a SaaS due diligence layer for corporate carbon procurement. Before a procurement team buys credits, they submit the project ID and vintage. CreditTrace runs an automated audit: satellite imagery comparison (is the forest still standing? has landcover changed?), cross-referencing IoT sensor data where available from the project, checking for double-counting across registries, and generating a plain-English risk score with a defensible PDF report suitable for auditors. It also tracks the credit through its lifecycle — retirement records, registry timestamps — and flags anomalies. Monthly SaaS for procurement teams, with per-credit report pricing for spot purchases.

**First wedge:** Fortune 500 sustainability and procurement teams at companies with high offset exposure — airlines, oil & gas companies with net-zero pledges, large consumer goods firms. Reach them through the Big 4 sustainability consulting practices (Deloitte, EY, KPMG) who are the ones being asked to sign off on these disclosures and currently have no tooling to back it up. Position as audit infrastructure, not green marketing.

**What kills it:** The voluntary carbon market could contract sharply if SEC enforcement creates a chilling effect on offset purchases entirely. You'd be selling shovels into a gold rush that might end. Also, the registries themselves could build verification APIs that commoditize part of the offering.

**Weirder variant:** Flip the model — sell CreditTrace to carbon project developers, not buyers. Developers who pass the audit get a "CreditTrace Verified" badge that commands a price premium in the market. It's a quality signal for the supply side, not a risk filter for the buy side.

---

## Where I Might Be Wrong

The weakest of the three is **CreditTrace** (#3). It assumes the voluntary carbon market stays large enough to justify enterprise SaaS procurement. If SEC enforcement makes CFOs reluctant to buy offsets at all — which is a live possibility — the TAM shrinks fast. The assumption that needs verification: are Fortune 500 sustainability teams *increasing* offset purchases in 2026 under regulatory pressure, or *decreasing* them to avoid audit risk? If the latter, CreditTrace is a product for a shrinking market.

**QueueDesk** (#1) has the clearest signal (2,060 GW in queue, informal trading already happening) but is most exposed to being built by a well-funded incumbent. The key bet is that Enverus, Wood Mackenzie, and BloombergNEF are all too slow and too focused on data subscriptions to build a transactions layer quickly.

**HeatOS** (#2) is probably the hardest to execute (SCADA integrations are brutal) but has the most durable moat if it works. The assumption that needs stress-testing: do plant operations managers have budget authority for a new software layer, or does this die in a 12-month procurement cycle?
