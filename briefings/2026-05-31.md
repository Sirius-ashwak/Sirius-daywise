## The 3 Ideas

---

### 1. Sanctioned

**One-line pitch:** Compliance-native deployment layer that gets AI agents approved in regulated industries.

**The specific problem:**
A hospital system's IT team deploys Microsoft Copilot. Agents work in staging. Then legal/compliance kills it — there's no way to prove the agent won't expose PHI, won't make autonomous clinical decisions, won't violate HIPAA audit requirements. Same story at banks (SOX), pharma (FDA 21 CFR Part 11), and insurers (state regulators). The agents are bought, licensed, configured — and sitting idle. IT leaders at 200-500 bed regional hospitals and $500M-$5B regional banks are the acute sufferers. They don't have in-house AI compliance teams. They're stuck.

**Why now:**
EU AI Act enforcement is live in 2026. FDA published its AI in clinical decision support guidance. SOX interpretations for autonomous systems are being written now, in real time. Compliance teams have never had to govern AI agents before and they're defaulting to "no." The regulatory pressure is creating a hard deadline for solutions.

**Mechanism:**
Sanctioned is a middleware layer that sits between your AI agents and your enterprise systems. Every agent action runs through a pre-execution compliance check against your specific regulatory framework (HIPAA, SOX, FDA, state insurance). It maintains immutable audit logs in regulator-expected formats. Provides pre-built compliance templates so legal teams can approve agent categories rather than individual prompts. Routes anything outside approved boundaries to human review via ServiceNow or Jira. Monthly compliance report auto-generated for auditors.

**First wedge:**
Regional hospital systems (200-500 beds), specifically their IT and compliance teams who already have Microsoft Copilot or Salesforce Agentforce licenses gathering dust. Reach them through HIMSS conference, healthcare IT buyer communities, and Microsoft/Salesforce partner channels (they want their agent licenses activated). Start with one workflow: clinical documentation AI agent approval.

**What kills it:**
Microsoft, Salesforce, or ServiceNow ships native compliance frameworks for their agent platforms, making the standalone middleware redundant. This is the real risk — big players have every incentive to unblock their own agent licenses.

**Weirder variant:**
Same product, repositioned for legal discovery. Law firms using AI to review documents need audit trails that hold up as evidence in court, not just internal compliance checkboxes. The "Sanctioned" layer becomes the chain of custody for AI-generated legal analysis.

### 2. Canopy

**One-line pitch:** Version control and rollback for AI agent decisions in enterprise workflows.

**The specific problem:**
Operations teams at mid-market companies (500–5,000 employees) are running AI agents across order processing, customer service resolution, vendor approvals, and HR document generation. When an agent makes a wrong call — cancels a $50K B2B order, approves a vendor it shouldn't, generates a wrong employment letter — there's no undo. No audit trail. No way to replay the scenario to see what a better-configured agent would have done. The pain is immediate and the blast radius is real. Ops and RevOps leads at e-commerce companies, logistics firms, and staffing agencies feel this most acutely.

**Why now:**
The Gartner 40%-by-end-of-2026 prediction means thousands of enterprises are deploying agents into production workflows right now, often for the first time. Failure modes are moving from hypothetical to actual. The orchestration platforms (LangGraph, Semantic Kernel, Bedrock Agents) give you agent deployment infrastructure — none of them give you recovery infrastructure.

**Mechanism:**
An SDK and thin infrastructure layer that wraps agent invocations. Before each agent action, Canopy captures a lightweight state snapshot of the affected systems (via API integrations with Salesforce, HubSpot, NetSuite, ServiceNow). If an action fails, gets flagged, or is disputed, ops can restore pre-action state, review the full decision trace (inputs, context window, tool calls, outputs), edit the agent config, and replay against the same original input to compare outcomes. Works across multi-agent pipelines, not just single agents.

**First wedge:**
E-commerce ops teams using agents for order exception handling — specifically Shopify Plus and BigCommerce enterprise merchants. When an agent wrongly processes a high-value B2B order, the dollar amount makes the pain concrete. Reach them through Shopify Plus partner channels and the e-commerce ops communities on Slack/LinkedIn.

**What kills it:**
The major orchestration platforms build rollback natively. This is more likely than it seems — it's table stakes infrastructure that every platform will eventually need. Canopy has to move fast and establish deep integrations before the platform players close the gap.

**Weirder variant:**
Reframe the product as a "regulatory defense layer." When a financial regulator asks "why did your AI system deny this loan applicant?", Canopy lets you replay the exact decision with the original inputs and produce an auditable reasoning chain. Same tech, different buyer — now you're selling to Chief Risk Officers, not ops teams.

### 3. DataVein

**One-line pitch:** Turns a vertical SaaS company's aggregated customer data into a benchmark revenue line.

**The specific problem:**
A vertical SaaS company with 4,000 restaurant customers has the most accurate restaurant operations benchmark data in the world. Their customers would pay to know: "How does my labor cost compare to similar-size restaurants in my city?" The SaaS company knows this — but they don't have the technical infrastructure to anonymize it properly, the legal framework to share it compliantly, or the product layer to sell it without alienating customers. So the data sits. Meanwhile, Veeva, Toast, Procore, and Mindbody are all sitting on similar goldmines they can't monetize. The product leaders and CDOs at vertical SaaS companies ($10M-$200M ARR) with large customer bases are the buyer.

**Why now:**
Privacy-preserving computation (differential privacy, synthetic data generation, secure multi-party computation) has matured to production-ready in 2026. Snowflake Horizon and Databricks Clean Rooms exist but are infrastructure primitives — they don't solve the product, legal, or go-to-market problem. EU AI Act and updated CCPA forced legal teams to actually build data-sharing frameworks, meaning the legal groundwork that previously didn't exist is now present in most mid-size SaaS companies.

**Mechanism:**
DataVein is a "SaaS for SaaS" product. Plug into the customer's data warehouse (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks). DataVein applies differential privacy transformations and generates benchmark datasets segmented by customer cohort, geography, and business size. Provides a white-label "Insights" module the SaaS company embeds in their own product — customers see their benchmarks without DataVein branding. Handles the consent flows, privacy compliance documentation, and audit trails. Revenue model: rev-share on the benchmark subscription the SaaS company sells to their customers.

**First wedge:**
Restaurant tech platforms in the Olo or Toast ecosystem with 1,000+ restaurant customers. Restaurant operators are obsessed with benchmarking (labor, food cost, table turns) — this is validated demand. Reach the vertical SaaS companies through their product and data leadership, not sales.

**What kills it:**
The vertical SaaS companies decide to build this in-house once you prove the concept. Your customer becomes your competitor. You need to move fast enough to become the standard infrastructure layer, not a one-off pilot.

**Weirder variant:**
Same infrastructure but sold to private equity firms for portfolio benchmarking. A PE firm with 15 portfolio companies across a sector wants normalized operational benchmarks across all of them — but the companies run on different ERPs. DataVein becomes the cross-portfolio data normalization and benchmarking layer.

## Where I Might Be Wrong

The weakest idea is **DataVein (#3)**. The thesis depends on vertical SaaS companies being willing to hand over data governance to a third party — but this is structurally the thing their legal and security teams resist most. Even with strong privacy tech, the "what if this goes wrong?" liability sits with the SaaS company, not DataVein. Also, Snowflake's Clean Rooms product is a direct competitor that already has trust with enterprise data teams. The market exists but the sales motion is harder than it looks.

**Sanctioned (#1)** has the opposite risk: the problem is real but healthcare and banking are notoriously slow buyers. A 14-month sales cycle at a regional hospital kills a seed-stage startup. The Microsoft/Salesforce partner channel angle might be the only viable go-to-market that compresses this.

**Canopy (#2)** has a technical assumption that needs verification: not all enterprise systems support clean state snapshots through their APIs. Salesforce, for example, doesn't have native event sourcing — reconstructing "state before this agent action" is genuinely hard. This needs a prototype before you raise on the idea.

The assumption most worth stress-testing across all three: are enterprises actually deploying agents into production fast enough in 2026 to create an urgent market for governance/recovery infrastructure? Or is the 11%-in-production number a leading indicator that adoption is actually slower than Gartner predicts?
