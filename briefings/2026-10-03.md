## Idea 1 — Residual
**Pitch:** Royalty meter that tracks and bills every time a brand's AI generates your likeness.

- **Problem:** Mid-tier creators (50k–2M followers) who signed a likeness/AI clause they didn't understand. A brand can now run unlimited synthetic ads in their voice and face; the creator got paid once.
- **Why now:** Perpetual AI-training clauses are now standard boilerplate, and tools like TikTok Symphony make generating "creator-style" ads a one-click action. The harm is live and measurable this year.
- **Mechanism:** Creator licenses a verified voice/face model through Residual instead of handing raw footage to brands. Brand generation tools call a metered API (or a plugin for ad platforms), each render is logged and billed per use, and the creator gets a statement. Contracts reference the license rather than granting rights outright.
- **First wedge:** Talent managers with 10–30 creators, who are already angry about these clauses. Reach them via the managers' Slack/Discords and a free "clause scanner" that flags perpetual AI rights in an uploaded contract.
- **What kills it:** Brands won't route generation through a third-party meter, and the big ad tools (TikTok, Meta) build their own creator-consent layer.
- **Weirder variant:** Deceased and retired creators' estates: Residual runs the likeness licence for a late creator's catalogue.

## Idea 2 — Unpolished
**Pitch:** Verified-human footage marketplace for brands that need authentic, imperfect creator content.

- **Problem:** Small D2C brands need UGC that doesn't read as AI slop, but can't tell what's real, and ad accounts get flagged or performance tanks when audiences smell synthetic.
- **Why now:** Audience trust in AI creator content has collapsed (26%), and brands are explicitly asking for messiness. Provenance standards (C2PA, platform AI labels) now make "shot by a real human on this date" provable.
- **Mechanism:** Creators shoot with a capture app that signs footage at the sensor level (device attestation plus location/time), then sell licensed raw clips and short edits. Brands filter by "verified-human" and get a provenance badge usable in ads and disclosures. Pricing is per clip with usage-based top-ups.
- **First wedge:** Shopify skincare and supplement brands doing $1–10M, who already buy UGC from Fiverr-style sources. Reach via Shopify agency communities and paid-ads Twitter.
- **What kills it:** Verification is easy to spoof or ignore, and audiences don't actually care about the badge. Cheap AI gets good enough that performance data stops favouring human footage.
- **Weirder variant:** Reverse it: the badge is sold to consumers, a browser extension that only shows reviews and product videos with human provenance.

## Idea 3 — Payday Pool
**Pitch:** Creators pool invoices from late-paying brands and borrow against them at pooled rates.

- **Problem:** Mid-tier creators wait 60–90 days for brand payments while paying editors and agents upfront, and individually they're too small and risky for factoring.
- **Why now:** Income and audience data is becoming API-accessible with creator consent (Phyllo-style rails), so a lender can underwrite a creator's real earnings history instead of guessing. Contract analysis by LLMs can read each deal for payment terms and clawback risk.
- **Mechanism:** Creator connects platform earnings and uploads signed brand contracts. The system scores each invoice by brand payment history and clause risk, then advances 80–90% of it from a pool funded by a small credit fund. Creators in the pool share the loss reserve, which lowers the rate for everyone.
- **First wedge:** Creators managed by small agencies (5–20 talent), where the agency vouches and aggregates. Reach through the agencies and creator-finance newsletters.
- **What kills it:** A few big brands default or dispute, the loss reserve blows up, and lending licensing and capital costs eat the margin before volume arrives.
- **Weirder variant:** Brands pay creators in the pool's token-free "net-0" credit instead: the pool pays creators immediately and brands pay the pool net-90 at a small premium, becoming a payments rail rather than a lender.

## Where I might be wrong
Payday Pool is the weakest: it's a lending business wearing a creator costume, and the loss-reserve maths only works if brand-default data is better than I'm assuming. Residual's core assumption, that brands will accept metered likeness licensing instead of a flat buyout, needs verifying with five talent managers before anything is built.
