## Idea 1: Liveproof
**One-line pitch:** Cryptographic proof of *who actually did the work* in a job application, not another AI detector.

**The problem:** Hiring managers can't tell human skill from AI-assisted output anymore, and the standard fixes (portfolios, take-homes, "vibe coding" sessions) are now just as gameable as the resume was. This hits technical recruiters and hiring managers at high-volume, digital-first employers hardest — they're the ones drowning in the 242-applications-per-posting flood and burning trust on both false positives and false negatives.

**Why now:** Coding/writing agents are ambient in every IDE, and so is the telemetry to watch them — in-editor session recording and keystroke/diff APIs are now standard infrastructure, not custom tooling. Two years ago you'd have had to build this instrumentation yourself; today it's a wrapper around what already ships.

**Mechanism:** A lightweight SDK sits in the candidate's own IDE/agent tools during a live take-home. It doesn't ban AI use — it timestamps every keystroke, agent prompt, and paste event against the final artifact, producing a "composition graph" that shows exactly what was hand-typed, agent-generated, or copied. Employers set their own thresholds instead of getting a binary yes/no.

**First wedge:** Dev-focused staffing platforms and bootcamps that already run take-homes and are bleeding credibility to fraud — sell it as an anti-fraud layer they resell into their enterprise placements.

**What kills it:** Candidates route around it with a second screen or device, and false accusations against juniors who used legitimate AI assistance trigger discrimination complaints and reputational backlash.

**Weirder variant:** Turn the composition graph inward — use it on your *own* employees' daily agent usage as a skill-retention signal for promotion and succession planning, not just at the hiring gate.

## Idea 2: Agent Range
**One-line pitch:** A flight simulator that trains staff to supervise *your* company's actual AI agents, not generic prompts.

**The problem:** Job postings now list titles like "AI Agent Orchestrator" and "Human-Agent Interaction Designer," but there's no training pipeline for them. Generic prompt-engineering courses don't touch a specific company's agent stack, permissions, or failure modes. Ops leaders at companies already running agentic workflows in support, finance, or dev feel this acutely — they're the 23% Gartner says are already scaling agentic systems in production.

**Why now:** Enterprise agent-orchestration platforms now expose replayable, inspectable logs and sandboxable environments. That's new — agents didn't have persistent, forkable state worth simulating until this generation of tooling.

**Mechanism:** Ingest a company's redacted agent-orchestration logs and replay realistic failure scenarios — a support agent hallucinating a refund, an ops agent escalating wrongly, a loop it can't break out of — inside a sandboxed clone of the real tools. Trainees practice catching and correcting the agent live, scored on time-to-catch and correction quality.

**First wedge:** Support-ops teams mid-transition from headcount to agents (the Salesforce 9,000-to-5,000 pattern) — sell to the ops leader who now has to turn the surviving staff into agent supervisors, priced per seat per quarter.

**What kills it:** Getting access to real, sensitive agent logs is a security and compliance minefield, and companies may just prefer hiring people who already know how to do this over building a simulator.

**Weirder variant:** Strip out the company-specific layer and sell a public certification built on aggregated, anonymized agent-failure patterns — a portable "can you catch a bad agent" credential individuals buy for themselves, independent of any employer.

## Idea 3: Understudy
**One-line pitch:** Junior hires get paid to supervise AI agents doing entry-level work, rebuilding the vanished first rung.

**The problem:** Entry-level jobs are the first casualty of agentic automation — Salesforce cut support headcount nearly in half by handing the work to agents. That kills the traditional way juniors learn a trade: by doing the grunt work under supervision. New grads and career-changers have no acute way in, even though WEF projects a net 78M new jobs by 2030 — the long-term demand isn't the problem, the on-ramp is.

**Why now:** This only works in a narrow window — agents good enough to do the grunt work but not reliable enough to run unsupervised. If agents get meaningfully better, the supervision layer disappears; if they weren't already this capable, there'd be nothing to supervise. That window is open right now, not two years ago and maybe not two years from now.

**Mechanism:** Understudy places a junior hire as the accountable human-in-the-loop over one agent workflow (support tickets, ad-ops, paralegal drafts) at a client company, paid partly by the client and partly by Understudy. The explicit deal: the junior's job is to get themselves promoted out of supervision within 6–12 months by documenting the agent's edge cases and improving it. Understudy takes a placement-plus-upskilling fee, not a permanent staffing cut.

**First wedge:** Mid-market companies that already cut junior headcount in favor of agents and are now discovering the agents drift without oversight — reachable through the ops leaders posting "AI Agent Orchestrator" reqs with no idea how to source or price the role.

**What kills it:** Companies just use a cheaper offshore contractor for the supervision role instead of paying a training premium, and the "promoted within a year" promise rings hollow if there's nowhere to promote to.

**Weirder variant:** Flip the accountability — the company hires the junior directly, but an Understudy-run agent watches the junior's work and is the one who escalates and corrects. It's an AI manager giving humans their on-the-job training back, instead of the other way around.

---

## Where I might be wrong

Understudy is the weakest of the three. It assumes companies want a deliberate transitional "supervision" role rather than skipping straight past it to full automation — but if frontier agent reliability is climbing faster than the market currently shows, that window may already be narrower than it looks, shrinking the pool of wedge companies rather than growing it. The thing to verify before building any of this: how many companies that cut junior headcount for agents are actually saying "our agents need a human catching mistakes" versus just quietly tightening QA in-house without hiring anyone new. If it's mostly the latter, Understudy has no market and Agent Range's enterprise-training angle becomes the stronger bet of the three.
