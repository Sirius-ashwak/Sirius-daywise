## The 3 Ideas

---

### 1. Meridian — Compliance middleware for AI hiring decisions

**One-line pitch:** Audit trail and explainability layer that sits on top of any ATS.

**The specific problem:**
Companies using AI-assisted hiring (resume screening, video interview scoring, automated ranking) cannot explain their decisions. When a candidate is rejected, neither the recruiter, the vendor, nor the company can reconstruct exactly which model, which signals, and what weights produced that outcome. EU AI Act (Dec 2027 deadline) requires full documentation and candidate-facing transparency for high-risk employment AI. US state laws in California, Colorado, Illinois are already live. Zero dedicated tooling exists — ATS vendors sell the hiring workflow, not the compliance layer on top of it.

**Why now:**
EU compliance deadline is fixed and known. The Dec 2027 date means procurement decisions need to happen in Q3/Q4 2026. State-level US laws (Colorado SB 205, NYC Local Law 144-style expansions) are already triggering EEOC and state AG enforcement. Fines under EU AI Act reach €15M or 3% of global turnover. This is no longer a "nice to have."

**Mechanism:**
Meridian is a middleware API that sits between any ATS (Greenhouse, Lever, Workday, iCIMS) and candidate decision events. At every automated decision point — score generated, candidate ranked, rejection triggered — Meridian captures the full decision chain: model version, feature weights, data inputs. It generates a human-readable candidate-facing explanation on demand (30-second setup per decision template) and packages the full audit trail for internal compliance review or legal defense. Integrates via webhook in under a day.

**First wedge:**
EU-based companies (500-2,000 employees) that are already using Greenhouse or Lever and have at least one GDPR compliance officer. These companies know the regulation is coming, have someone internally responsible, and lack budget for a custom build. Reach them through HR compliance law firms (who need to recommend a solution to clients) and through the HR-ON, Boundless, and Omni community audiences who are actively publishing EU AI Act compliance content.

**What kills it:**
Greenhouse, Lever, and Workday build this natively in 18 months. They will. But it'll be buried in their enterprise tier, take 2 years to ship properly, and be unusable for companies running multi-vendor stacks. The window for an independent layer is real but finite — probably 3-4 years.

**Weirder variant:**
Flip it candidate-facing first. A browser extension that shows applicants their estimated AI score in real-time as they apply, auto-detects which ATS is in use, and flags potential bias patterns. Consumer distribution, viral mechanic, B2B compliance upsell after adoption.

### 2. Cartograph — Async work evidence for people who write their own reviews

**One-line pitch:** Captures micro-evidence of your work so you're not guessing at review time.

**The specific problem:**
98% of CHROs hate performance reviews. The mechanism of failure is specific: performance reviews are memory-based, and human memory is biased, incomplete, and recency-weighted. Remote and hybrid workers are especially disadvantaged — managers literally don't see the work. Employees spend 3-8 hours writing self-assessments twice a year, starting from a blank document and reconstructing the previous 6 months from Slack search and calendar. The output is political theater, not evidence.

**Why now:**
LLMs can now categorize and structure qualitative work evidence without hallucinating the substance — they just organize what's actually there. Privacy-safe local-first architectures (similar to Obsidian's model) mean employees can own their data without creating a surveillance product. The skills-based hiring wave (SkillVector, etc.) means "proof of work" is increasingly machine-readable and portable — your work log becomes a career asset, not just a review prep tool.

**Mechanism:**
Cartograph is a lightweight capture layer that integrates with Slack, Linear, GitHub, Notion, and email. It runs locally or in a privacy-safe personal data store (employee-owned, not company-visible). When you ship something, close a ticket, unblock a teammate, or get a positive mention in Slack, it captures a timestamped micro-log — not surveillance, just structured metadata about work events you initiated. At review time, the AI organizes these entries into a structured self-assessment draft: impact claims backed by actual references, not reconstructed from memory. Employees can export and own the full record.

**First wedge:**
Individual contributors at remote-first companies — specifically engineers and PMs who already write their own perf reviews. Sell it direct-to-employee at $10/month as a "career OS." Don't pitch HR first. Wait for bottom-up adoption, then offer HR the aggregate dashboard (anonymized, team-level) to understand which teams are shipping what. That's the B2B upsell.

**What kills it:**
Habit formation. This is the graveyard where daily journaling apps go to die. If capture requires any intentional action, most people won't do it during the busy periods when it would be most valuable. The only way this works is if 90% of capture is fully passive. Verify that before building.

**Weirder variant:**
Reposition as portable work reputation — a professional record that travels with you between jobs. Your Cartograph becomes the proof layer for your LinkedIn claims. Companies can request verified work evidence instead of reference calls. You're building the "employment history" that actually shows the work.

### 3. Manifold — Accountability layer for AI agents acting in workforce roles

**One-line pitch:** Tracks which tasks in your company are done by humans vs. AI agents, with full audit trail.

**The specific problem:**
In 2026, thousands of companies have deployed AI agents performing tasks that used to be human jobs: triaging customer complaints, flagging compliance issues, pre-screening candidates, generating code that ships to production. HR and legal infrastructure is entirely built for human workers. When an AI agent makes a wrong call — misses a compliance flag, makes a discriminatory recommendation, takes an unauthorized action — no system in the company tracks who (or what) was responsible. EEOC has stated clearly that employers bear liability for AI outcomes. D&O insurers are starting to ask for AI governance documentation. Courts are rejecting the "the AI did it" defense.

**Why now:**
AI agent deployments at mid-size companies accelerated through 2025. Governance tooling is 2+ years behind. The liability is landing now: hundreds of EEOC complaints tied to AI hiring in 2024, state AGs in California and Illinois actively investigating automated employment decisions. Insurance carriers are the forcing function — they'll start requiring AI governance audits before underwriting D&O and employment practices liability policies.

**Mechanism:**
Manifold is an integration layer that connects to your existing workflow tools (Zapier, Make, n8n, your internal orchestration) and registers every task execution — what was done, by whom (human role or AI agent ID), what model/version, what data inputs, what decision or output. It maps your "AI workforce footprint." Generates compliance reports in formats required by EU AI Act, EEOC audit requests, and insurer risk assessments. Alerts when AI agents are taking actions outside their designated scope (e.g., an agent trained for triage starts making final reject decisions).

**First wedge:**
Financial services and healthcare companies that have already deployed AI agents for customer-facing workflows and recently received a compliance inquiry or audit request. These companies have the most to lose and the most internal urgency. Reach them via risk management consultants and insurance brokers who are already in the conversation around AI liability — they need a product to recommend, not just a framework to write.

**What kills it:**
Positioning. "AI governance compliance tool" sounds like something a VP of Risk buys once and never uses. It needs a sharp hook that creates urgency and a champion. The insurance angle (start with EPL policy underwriting requirements) or the EEOC defense package (audit-ready in 48 hours) are better wedges than "governance platform."

**Weirder variant:**
Build it as the insurance product, not the software. Underwrite "AI workforce liability" directly — your software is how you assess and price the risk. You're Lloyd's of London for AI agent mishaps. Premium scales with the scope of AI decision-making in their workforce. The tech company is the risk model; the insurance is the distribution.

## Where I Might Be Wrong

The weakest idea is **#2 (Cartograph)**. The graveyard of digital journaling and work logging tools is large — Weekdone, iDoneThis, journal apps with Slack integrations — and they all died because capturing consistently during busy periods is exactly when people stop. The assumption that needs validation before building: do employees at remote-first companies actually spend 3+ hours writing self-assessments twice a year? If yes, that acute pain is the behavior-change trigger. If the actual behavior is "spend 90 minutes dashing something off," the pain isn't sharp enough to change habits.

**#1 (Meridian)** is the strongest because the regulatory forcing function is external, fixed, and increasingly well-understood by buyers — Dec 2027 isn't moving. The risk is that ATS incumbents move faster than expected; watch Greenhouse's product roadmap.

**#3 (Manifold)** is genuinely novel but may be 12-18 months early. Enterprise risk buyers are slow, and the insurance angle — while compelling — requires building a licensed insurance product, which is a completely different company. The pure-software version needs a cleaner, more urgent hook to avoid dying in 18-month enterprise sales cycles.
