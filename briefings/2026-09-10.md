## Idea 1 — Proofline

**Pitch:** Skill verification pulled from the artifacts of real work, not staged tests AI can already fake.

**The problem:** Every technical hiring pipeline in 2026 is gameable — take-homes, live coding rounds, even video interviews get an AI whisper-assist. Engineering managers at Series A–C startups know their funnel is full of candidates who interview well and can't actually debug. This is the exact "verification is the bottleneck" complaint dominating HN right now.

**Why now:** The interview-cheating tooling (real-time AI overlays, whisper coaching) went mainstream in the last year, faster than any interview format could adapt. At the same time, every candidate now has a rich, timestamped digital trail — git history, PR review threads, IDE session replays — that's far harder to fake convincingly than a 45-minute call.

**Mechanism:** Proofline plugs into a candidate's (consented) git/PR history and, during a final-round exercise, records IDE telemetry — edit velocity, revision/backtrack patterns, time-to-first-keystroke after reading a bug report — that AI-assisted work has a distinct signature for. It cross-references against their real commit history to flag "this looks like a different skill profile than their own repo history," not just "this looks AI-written."

**First wedge:** Seed-to-Series-B startups doing high-volume engineering hiring (50+ interviews/quarter) who are already burned by bad hires post-interview. Reach them through the same YC/founder Slack and Twitter/X channels where "our hiring pipeline is broken" is already a recurring complaint.

**What kills it:** Candidates route around it (do the exercise in an environment Proofline can't instrument), or it becomes a two-week arms race against whatever the next AI-assist tool does — same fate as anti-cheat in every other domain. Also a hard sell if it feels like surveillance to candidates in a tight talent market.

**Weirder variant:** Point the same "authorship forensics from revision history" engine at college admissions essays and fellowship applications — sold to selective programs (not as a cheating cop, but as a "show us your real thinking" tool applicants opt into for a leg up), using Google Docs' own version history as the artifact trail instead of git.

## Idea 2 — Halflife

**Pitch:** Flags exactly which skills your team is quietly losing to AI assistance, before an outage proves it.

**The problem:** Engineering leads and CTOs increasingly worry (per the HN/HackerNoon thread this week) that juniors leaning on Copilot-style tools can ship code they can't explain or debug unassisted. Nobody currently measures this — LMS completion and course assignments don't reflect what an engineer can do when the assistant is down.

**Why now:** Two years of heavy AI-assisted coding have produced a cohort of engineers who've never had to debug "cold" at scale, right as the training/L&D funding wave (Multiverse, Guild, BetterUp, Go1 — $4.6B combined) makes clear enterprises are willing to spend serious money on workforce capability, if it's tied to a measurable outcome rather than seat-time.

**Mechanism:** Halflife reads existing engineering telemetry orgs already have — PR review comments, time-to-resolution on tickets, Copilot/agent acceptance rates per engineer — to build a per-person, per-skill decay curve (e.g., "hasn't manually debugged a race condition without AI assist in 4 months"). It then auto-injects short, real (not simulated) "manual mode" tasks pulled from the org's own backlog to keep specific skills live, rather than assigning generic courses.

**First wedge:** Engineering managers at dev agencies and consultancies, where client work demands provable human competence (contracts, compliance, insurance) independent of tooling — sell it as risk mitigation, not training software, and reach them through the same channels running the "juniors can't debug" conversation on HN and X.

**What kills it:** Feels punitive if positioned wrong ("we're monitoring how much you rely on AI") and triggers exactly the kind of employee pushback that killed keystroke-monitoring products. Also depends on integrating with tools (Copilot, Cursor, internal agent platforms) that may not expose usage data cleanly.

**Weirder variant:** Package the aggregate decay score as "skill continuity insurance" — an actuarial product enterprises buy so that if their agent stack goes down for a day, an insurer has already verified what percent of the affected team can still do the work manually, and prices business-interruption coverage accordingly.

## Idea 3 — Personnel File (for agents)

**Pitch:** The HR department nobody's built yet — but for your company's AI agents, not your humans.

**The problem:** As of this week, agents are starting to hold real binding authority (Docusign opening MCP to all agents on Sept 30) and enterprises are standing up dedicated human teams just to deploy and manage agent workforces (Accenture/Google's 1,000-person group). But when an agent screws up a contract, approves a bad invoice, or needs to be "let go" and replaced with a new version, there's no equivalent of an employee file, performance review, or documented off-boarding — just scattered logs across whatever MCP servers it touched.

**Why now:** MCP has quietly become the plumbing that gives agents persistent tool access and identity across systems this year, and the market is visibly nervous about it — ServiceNow just bought an agent-ops startup and Palo Alto paid $500M for an agent-security one. Governance spend is happening now; nobody's framed it in the compliance language auditors and boards already understand (an "HR file"), which is the fastest way to get budget signed off without inventing a new category.

**Mechanism:** Personnel File ingests tool-call logs across an org's MCP-connected agent fleet and builds a per-agent-identity record: what decisions it made, what it was authorized to do, every human override or escalation, and a running "performance review" score. When an agent version is retired or its permissions revoked, it generates the audit trail as a formal "termination record" — mapped directly onto compliance frameworks auditors already use for human staff with signing authority.

**First wedge:** Mid-market insurance and fintech companies rolling out their first Docusign-MCP-connected approval agents this quarter, who are already asking their compliance teams "what happens when this agent signs something wrong" — reach them through the systems integrators (Accenture-style partners) doing the actual agent rollouts, as an add-on to the deployment.

**What kills it:** Regulation moves first and a specific audit-log format gets mandated (EU AI Act-style), which ServiceNow, Salesforce, or Microsoft then bake into their existing platforms for free — killing the point-solution wedge before it scales. Also possible the market files this under "security/observability," not "HR," and never buys the framing at all.

**Weirder variant:** A consumer version for people running a growing swarm of personal agents (inbox, calendar, shopping, bill-pay) — a personal "HR dashboard" for your own AI staff, with a one-click "performance review → fire and replace" flow when one of them screws up your grocery order or double-books a flight.

## Where I might be wrong

**Idea 3 is the weakest of the three**, precisely because it's the one I like most. The core bet — that enterprises will conceptualize agent accountability as an *HR* problem rather than folding it into existing security/observability/GRC spend — is unverified and could just be wrong; ServiceNow and Palo Alto's recent acquisitions suggest the market is already filing this under "security," which would make the HR framing a marketing mismatch, not a wedge. The assumption to test first: talk to 5–10 compliance leads at companies actually turning on Docusign's new agent access this month, and see which department signs the check.

Idea 1 (Proofline) has the most competition already (skills-verification is a crowded, half-dead category from the last cycle) — its differentiation lives entirely in the "artifact forensics vs. staged test" mechanism, which needs a real false-positive/negative rate before it's fundable, not just a good pitch.
