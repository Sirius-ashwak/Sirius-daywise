## Three ideas

### 1. Scoped
**Pitch:** A HIPAA-safe runtime that lets doctors ship the tools they vibe-coded without IT saying no.

**Problem:** Physicians are now building single-purpose clinical apps (a rounding checklist, a discharge-summary formatter, a specialty-specific calculator) with Claude Code / Cursor in an afternoon. Health system IT can't evaluate, sandbox, or approve these fast enough — so 99% never leave the doctor's laptop, or worse, get used ad hoc against PHI with zero governance. The acute sufferer: a CMIO or health-system IT security lead who is being asked by 50 different doctors to "just deploy this thing I built."

**Why now:** Agentic coding tools crossed the threshold in 2026 where a non-engineer clinician can produce a working, reasonably correct app in under a day. That capability didn't exist 18 months ago. Meanwhile HIPAA/SOC2 tooling (policy-as-code, PHI redaction proxies, audit-log-by-default runtimes) matured enough to bolt onto arbitrary code rather than requiring a bespoke build.

**Mechanism:** Doctor builds their tool locally with whatever AI coding assistant they like. They push it to Scoped instead of a personal server. Scoped runs it in a locked-down container with a PHI firewall (structured data in/out only, no raw EHR scraping), auto-generates the audit trail a compliance officer needs, and gives IT a one-click kill switch and review queue instead of a six-month app-review process.

**First wedge:** Don't sell to the health system first — sell to the doctor. $49/month, "deploy your side project safely," targeted at the exact clinicians showing up in Claude Code / Anthropic healthcare webinars and on r/medicine already bragging about what they built. Once 10+ doctors in one hospital are using it, the compliance conversation becomes inbound instead of outbound.

**What kills it:** Health systems decide the liability is too radioactive regardless of the sandbox, and simply ban unsanctioned clinician-built tools outright rather than adopt a safe-deployment layer — killing the wedge before it can climb to the enterprise sale.

**Weirder variant:** Same runtime, but for veterinary practices — where there's no HIPAA-equivalent barrier, adoption could be 10x faster, and it becomes the proving ground before going back to human health systems.

### 2. Causal Ring
**Pitch:** Turns your wearable's years of data into an N-of-1 experiment engine for what actually extends your healthspan.

**Problem:** Millions of people now have 2-4 years of continuous biosensor data (Oura, CGMs, Whoop) sitting unused. They try interventions — creatine, cold plunges, a new sleep schedule — but have no rigorous way to know if it worked for *them specifically*, versus population-level studies that don't apply to their body. The acute sufferer: the longevity-obsessed but not-a-data-scientist consumer who's spending $500+/month on Function Health-style panels and supplements with no causal feedback loop.

**Why now:** Two things converged this year: (1) wearable makers like Oura are sitting on genuinely massive longitudinal datasets and are under investor pressure (post their $11B raise) to prove clinical value beyond "cute ring," and (2) foundation models got good enough at time-series causal inference (change-point detection, synthetic-control methods) to run defensible per-person analysis without a data scientist in the loop.

**Mechanism:** User connects their wearable(s) and logs interventions in a dead-simple way (a text message: "started magnesium glycinate today"). The model treats each intervention as a natural experiment against the user's own historical baseline, using synthetic-control techniques to strip out confounders (illness, travel, season), and reports back in plain language whether the intervention moved a specific biomarker outside their own noise band — with honest confidence intervals, not vibes.

**First wedge:** The biohacker forums and Reddit's r/longevity/r/quantifiedself crowd already track this manually in spreadsheets — sell them the automated version first, hand-hold the first 100 users, and use their (consented) data to validate the causal engine before touching a wearable-company partnership.

**What kills it:** Individual physiological noise is often larger than any real intervention effect, so the tool either produces "not enough data" constantly (frustrating) or false positives (dangerous) — and there's no easy way to know which failure mode you're in until users have already churned.

**Weirder variant:** Point the same causal engine at pets — a huge and underserved wearable market (dog/cat activity collars) where owners will happily run N-of-1 diet and supplement experiments they'd never approve on themselves.

### 3. Arbiter
**Pitch:** A neutral clearinghouse where payer denial-bots and provider appeal-bots settle claims instead of escalating forever.

**Problem:** Payers now run AI agents to deny claims at scale (this is explicitly why Amperos Health and Penguin Ai exist — to fight back on the provider side with AI-generated appeals). Both sides are now automating an adversarial process that used to be rate-limited by human labor. The acute sufferer: mid-size hospital billing departments about to get buried under a denial volume that scales with the payer's compute budget, not clinical merit.

**Why now:** This is a 2026-specific failure mode — it literally could not exist before both sides had deployed production LLM agents into the claims pipeline in the same year. Regulators (CMS prior-auth AI rules moving through rulemaking this year) are also actively looking for a place to put a human-in-the-loop checkpoint, which a neutral clearinghouse is well positioned to become.

**Mechanism:** Arbiter sits between payer and provider systems as an API both sides' bots already have to call anyway (most denial/appeal AI products need somewhere to submit to). It structures the negotiation as a documented, auditable exchange — each side's AI has to cite the specific clinical policy or documentation it's relying on — and escalates only genuine disputes to a human adjudicator, cutting the back-and-forth that currently burns weeks.

**First wedge:** Don't try to sign payers and providers simultaneously — build it as a provider-side tool first (a smarter version of what Amperos/Penguin already do) that happens to produce clean structured output a payer's system could consume, and let the clearinghouse layer emerge once enough providers are running through it that payers want the standardized interface too.

**What kills it:** Payers have no incentive to make their own denial bots easier to argue with — the entire point of their automation is to increase float and reduce payout, so a "fairer, faster" clearinghouse might get provider adoption but never payer buy-in, capping it as a one-sided tool forever.

**Weirder variant:** Take the same "AI vs AI needs a referee" mechanism out of healthcare entirely and into insurance claims generally — auto insurance total-loss disputes are already heading the same direction.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Arbiter** is the weakest of the three — it depends on an assumption I haven't verified: that payers' denial AI and providers' appeal AI are actually interacting adversarially at meaningful volume *today*, rather than this being two separate trends I'm pattern-matching into a conflict. If payer-side denial automation is still mostly rules-based (not LLM-agentic) in 2026, the "AI vs AI arms race" framing collapses and Arbiter is solving a problem that's 2-3 years early. Also worth checking: whether CMS's actual prior-auth rulemaking creates space for a private clearinghouse or forces this function into a government-run utility instead, which would kill the business model outright.
