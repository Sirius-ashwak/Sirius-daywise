## Three ideas

### 1. Queuehouse
**Pitch:** A secondary market and risk-scoring engine for grid interconnection queue positions.

**The problem:** Developers sit on interconnection queue slots worth enormously different amounts depending on utility, position, and study status — but there's no efficient way to price, verify, or transfer them. Right now this happens informally: PE firms quietly buy shell companies that hold good queue positions. That's inefficient and opaque, and it's exactly why 80% of the queue is dead weight nobody will let go of cleanly.

**Why now:** The queue backlog is bad enough in 2026 that positions themselves have become the scarce asset, not the generation hardware. FERC's queue reform (first-ready, first-served processing) is also making queue standing something you can actually score and rank instead of just guess at.

**Mechanism:** Ingest utility interconnection study data, generator interconnection agreements, and public FERC/ISO filings to build a live model of every queue position's expected time-to-energization and probability of survival. List positions for sale or lease with that score attached, handle the utility-side transfer paperwork, and take a cut on transacted deals.

**First wedge:** Start with ERCOT's large-load queue (226 GW and growing) and the data center developers already flipping shell entities informally — give them pricing data and a cleaner transaction rail than lawyers doing it deal-by-deal.

**What kills it:** Interconnection rights are often explicitly non-transferable under a utility's tariff, or transfer resets you to the back of the queue — if that's true in most jurisdictions, there's no asset to trade, just an information product.

**Weirder variant:** Push it down to the residential level — a "Polymarket for your solar interconnection" where homeowners and installers can bet on and hedge against permitting/interconnection delays, monetizing the wait itself instead of just suffering through it.

---

### 2. GridProof
**Pitch:** Title insurance for solar and storage — verifies your system is actually interconnected, not just installed.

**The problem:** The gap between "panels are on the roof" and "system is legally interconnected and producing" is where homeowners get destroyed — paying full loan payments on systems that are permitted-but-dead or never inspected. Financiers underwrite the loan, not the interconnection status, so nobody catches it until the homeowner notices their bill didn't drop.

**Why now:** Solar+storage financing volume is high enough, and horror stories viral enough (Reddit, local news) in 2026 that lenders and title/escrow-adjacent players are actively looking for a de-risking layer, the same way mortgage title insurance emerged once home sales got too complex for buyers to self-verify.

**Mechanism:** Pull utility interconnection agreement status, PTO (permission-to-operate) records, and production data via utility APIs and smart meter feeds, cross-check against the loan/financing agreement, and flag mismatches in real time. Sell it as a bolt-on insurance product to solar lenders and as a standalone policy to homeowners at close.

**First wedge:** Partner with 2-3 solar financing companies who've been burned by installer fraud/abandonment and want a covenant they can point to when underwriting new loans.

**What kills it:** Utility APIs for PTO/interconnection status are inconsistent to nonexistent across the ~3,000 US utilities — this could turn into a manual-verification business that doesn't scale, which is a services company wearing a software company's clothes.

**Weirder variant:** Extend the same "verify it actually works" insurance to VPP enrollment — PG&E's SHARE and Base Power are promising utilities dispatchable capacity from thousands of home batteries; sell the utility a policy that pays out if the promised megawatts don't show up when called.

---

### 3. Genset Exchange
**Pitch:** A leasing marketplace for modular behind-the-meter power, for AI/colo developers too small to build their own gas plant.

**The problem:** The behind-the-meter gold rush (xAI, DayOne, Nscale) is a capital-intensive game only hyperscalers can play — gigawatt gas turbines have 128-week lead times and require the kind of balance sheet only a handful of companies have. Sub-50MW colo and edge-inference developers face the exact same grid delays with none of that leverage.

**Why now:** There's now a real secondary supply of used/modular gas turbines, fuel cells, and mobile generators being displaced as bigger players upgrade, plus enough behind-the-meter precedent set by the majors that utilities and regulators have stopped treating on-site generation as exotic.

**Mechanism:** Aggregate and broker modular generation equipment (used turbines, containerized fuel cells, mobile gensets) with financing and siting/permitting support bundled in, deployable in weeks rather than years, sized for 5-50MW loads instead of gigawatt campuses.

**First wedge:** Edge AI inference and regional colo operators in interconnection-constrained markets (Northern Virginia, ERCOT) who are being quoted 5+ year utility timelines and have no in-house power development team.

**What kills it:** Fuel supply and emissions permitting for on-site gas at this scale can be just as slow as grid interconnection in many states — you might just be trading one queue for another.

**Weirder variant:** Same brokered-modular-power model, but for disaster response and forward military logistics — climate disasters are creating recurring, predictable demand spikes for fast-deploy power that this equipment pool is already built to serve.

## Where I might be wrong

**Queuehouse is the weakest of the three.** Its entire premise depends on interconnection queue positions being a transferable asset, and in a lot of jurisdictions they explicitly aren't — utilities structure interconnection agreements to be non-assignable specifically to prevent queue squatting and flipping. If that's the case everywhere, this collapses from "marketplace" into "just a data/analytics product," which is a much smaller business. Before going further I'd want to check actual FERC/ISO tariff language on assignability in at least ERCOT, PJM, and CAISO, and find out how the informal shell-company transfers people are already doing actually work legally.

GridProof and Genset Exchange are more grounded — I found direct, specific pain (the stranded-solar homeowner case, the 128-week turbine lead times) rather than inferring it.
