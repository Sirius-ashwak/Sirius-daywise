## Three ideas

### 1. Creditline
**One-line pitch:** A signed "influence receipt" that survives the hop from creator content to AI-agent checkout, so commissions don't vanish.

**The problem:** Affiliate/commerce creators — the beauty, supplement, and gadget reviewers whose entire income is commission-based — are losing credit the moment a viewer's AI shopping agent (Gemini, Rufus, Sparky) completes the purchase instead of clicking the link. Last-click attribution simply doesn't see the agent hop.

**Why now:** Google's Universal Commerce Protocol just launched and defines product feeds, carts, identity, and checkout — but the spec is young enough that a referrer/attribution extension isn't locked in yet. This is a narrow window to get a "creator credit" field adopted before UCP calcifies as the de facto standard.

**Mechanism:** Creators embed a lightweight pixel/webhook on their content. When a fan's AI agent ingests that content and later checks out via UCP, Creditline stamps a signed influence receipt into the agent's checkout payload as a custom field. Brands that adopt it settle payouts in real time off a ledger instead of a dying affiliate-link click.

**First wedge:** Messy v1 for mid-tier beauty/supplement affiliates already burned by falling commissions — ship a Chrome extension that generates "agent-proof" links today (works around the standard) while pushing for the receipt format to get adopted upstream. Distribute through affiliate-creator Discords and the brands (Whole Truth, Nua) already paying out high commissions.

**What kills it:** Zero incentive for the platforms that control checkout (Amazon, OpenAI, Google) to close a loop that benefits creators and brands but not them. If UCP finalizes with no extension mechanism, there's no lever left to pull.

**Weirder variant:** Same influence-receipt idea for B2B — a sales rep's LinkedIn post earns credit when an AI procurement agent evaluates and picks their vendor.

---

### 2. Proxy
**One-line pitch:** Creators license a guardrailed clone of themselves that other brands and creators can rent by the task.

**The problem:** Nano/micro creators (1K–50K followers) get more UGC and brand-deal inbound than they can personally fulfill, and they have no way to monetize their style/voice beyond their own limited hours.

**Why now:** Picsart's AI agent marketplace just proved platforms will host "hire an agent" as a real product category, and voice/likeness digital-replica licensing frameworks matured enough in 2025–26 that a creator can grant scoped, revocable rights to their own clone.

**Mechanism:** A creator uploads training material (voice, captions, past UGC) to build a licensed agent with hard guardrails they set. Brands or bigger creators rent it per task — "write 10 captions in this style," "have this agent handle routine UGC-brief negotiation within these limits" — and the creator takes a cut without doing the work personally.

**First wedge:** Micro-creators drowning in low-value UGC brand requests they currently decline or half-ass. Reach them through the UGC-marketplace platforms (Billo-style, brand-deal Discords) they already use to find gigs, offering Proxy as the way to say yes to more requests.

**What kills it:** An autonomous agent negotiating on a creator's behalf and making one bad call (over-promising, off-brand tone) can torch a real relationship or the creator's reputation — the liability is personal and hard to undo. Platform ToS around automated DMs (Instagram, TikTok) is also a live risk.

**Weirder variant:** Politicians or execs license a "constituent-service agent" trained on their public statements to handle town-hall-style Q&A at scale.

---

### 3. Dropfund
**One-line pitch:** Fans pre-fund a creator's next physical product drop directly at checkout, in exchange for a real revenue share.

**The problem:** Creators launching their first physical SKU (skincare, supplements, apparel — the exact category Ultrahuman/Whole Truth/Nua scaled in) need $20–50K for a production run but can't get VC-scale funding pre-revenue, so they either bootstrap slowly or take predatory creator cash-advance loans.

**Why now:** Embedded-finance and digital-securities rails matured enough by 2025–26 that compliant revenue-share notes can be issued to a fan base in minutes, not months. Meanwhile D2C investors are explicitly demanding "real revenue, clear path to profit" before writing checks — Dropfund lets a creator generate exactly that proof through fan pre-commitment before ever pitching a VC.

**Mechanism:** A creator connects Shopify/TikTok Shop, picks a SKU, sets a raise target and revenue-share terms. Fans buy "founding units" — part pre-order, part revenue-share note — directly in checkout. Funds sit in production escrow until the MOQ threshold hits; revenue share auto-pays fans via Stripe Connect against actual sales reported from that same checkout stack.

**First wedge:** Micro-brand creators already running informal "waitlist deposit" drops by DM and spreadsheet — replace the chaos with one embeddable Shopify widget. Distribute through the Shopify app store and D2C-creator Slack/Discord communities.

**What kills it:** Securities regulation varies wildly by country and is the whole ballgame here — get it wrong and this is a compliance nightmare. If the product flops, fans lose real money and it becomes a fast, ugly, personal reputational crisis for the creator, not just a failed startup.

**Weirder variant:** Apply it to physical local commerce — neighborhood regulars fund the expansion of their favorite creator-owned coffee shop for a slice of revenue, community-owned local retail.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Dropfund is the weakest of the three.** The entire mechanism leans on an assumption I haven't verified: that compliant, embeddable revenue-share/crowdfunding rails actually exist as a pluggable API product in 2026, cheaply enough for a $30K raise to pencil out after compliance overhead. If that infra isn't there yet, this is a fintech-and-legal build, not a wrapper. Creditline has a smaller version of the same risk — it bets that UCP will accept third-party extension fields at all; if Google locks the spec down without an extension point, there's no wedge left. Proxy is the most buildable of the three but the one most exposed to reputational blowback from a single bad agent action, which is hard to underwrite in advance.
