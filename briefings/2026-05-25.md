## The 3 Ideas

---

### 1. AgeStack
**One-line pitch:** Plaid for aging clocks — translate competing biological age data into one comparable standard.

**The specific problem:**
Retro, NewLimit, Altos, and every longevity clinical-stage company uses a different proprietary aging clock (DunedinPACE, GrimAge, PhenoAge, or their own). A trial sponsor can't compare their Phase 2 results to a competitor's. Academic researchers can't meta-analyze across studies. The FDA has no standard endpoint for "biological age." The field just agreed on 14 consensus biomarkers in 2026 — but no one has built the middleware to enforce or translate to that standard.

**Why now:**
Nature published the first formal recommendations for standardized biomarker data collection in longevity trials in early 2026. The field finally has consensus. FDA is being pushed to define biological age as a valid trial endpoint. The data lake is being built — the interoperability layer doesn't exist yet.

**Mechanism:**
API that ingests raw biomarker outputs from labs (Quest, LabCorp, GRAIL, Viome), longevity platforms (Function Health, Neko), and proprietary trial clocks. Normalizes everything to the 14-consensus-marker framework. Outputs a standardized biological age delta and confidence interval. Trial sponsors plug in at the CRO level; researchers get comparable cross-study data. Regulatory submission packages auto-formatted for FDA's emerging biological age endpoint requirements.

**First wedge:**
Series A-C longevity biotechs who need to demonstrate biomarker efficacy to raise their next round or file an IND. They're desperate for something FDA will accept. Reach them through longevity VC offices (Khosla, a16z Bio) and the CROs running their trials.

**What kills it:**
One of the big players (Altos, Retro, NewLimit) or a NIH/NIA consortium publishes an open standard and tooling. Or the biotechs prefer proprietary clocks because that's where their IP lives — no one wants to be commoditized.

**Weirder variant:**
Consumer-facing biological age credit score — aggregates Oura, Function Health, Apple Health, Viome into one standardized biological age score you share with your insurer or employer's wellness program for premium discounts. Terrifying, probably inevitable, and already happening in pieces in Japan.

### 2. Span
**One-line pitch:** Clinical decision support so primary care docs can actually act on their patients' longevity data.

**The specific problem:**
A patient walks into their 12-minute primary care visit with 300 biomarkers from Function Health, a Neko full-body scan, CGM data from a Levels subscription, and their Oura ring report. Their doctor has no training in longevity medicine and no tools to synthesize this. Nothing gets actioned. The "implementation gap" — having data but not changing healthspan outcomes — is the core problem right now. Function's Ezra acquisition makes this worse: more data, same broken clinical interface.

**Why now:**
Function Health + Ezra just created a massive data flywheel pointing at the doctor-patient interface. There are tens of thousands of patients generating sophisticated longevity data with no clinical pathway for it. Simultaneously, AI diagnostic synthesis (clinical-grade LLMs fine-tuned on longitudinal health data) is good enough in 2026 to prioritize across multi-modal inputs in real-time.

**Mechanism:**
EMR plugin for Epic and Athena that pulls in external longevity data via patient-authorized APIs (Function Health, Oura, Levels, Neko). Before the appointment, it surfaces a ranked list of 3 actionable items for the physician — ranked by evidence quality, patient risk, and visit time required. Not a diagnosis, not a treatment plan — a synthesis layer that turns 300 data points into "here's what to talk about today and why." Liability stays with the doctor; the tool just eliminates the overwhelm.

**First wedge:**
Concierge medicine practices (Parsley Health, MDVIP, direct-pay primary care) getting crushed with longevity-curious patients who've already bought the full tech stack. They're paying for premium clinical tools already and have 45-minute visits where this actually matters. Reach them at A4M (American Academy of Anti-Aging Medicine) conference and longevity medicine CME programs.

**What kills it:**
Epic builds this natively (they're already doing AI summarization). Or Function Health goes direct-to-consumer so aggressively they cut the doctor out entirely and build their own care layer. Or physician liability concerns around any AI recommendation create a compliance wall.

**Weirder variant:**
Flip it to the patient side: an AI longevity advocate that sits between your data stack and your insurance company. It mines your biological data to proactively petition for coverage of longevity interventions — "your biological age is 42 but chronological age is 51, here's a coverage request for GLP-1 therapy and continuous CGM." Startup as insurance negotiator.

### 3. TrialSense
**One-line pitch:** IRB-ready continuous digital biomarker collection platform for decentralized longevity clinical trials.

**The specific problem:**
Running a longevity clinical trial costs $50K+ per patient in site visits, phlebotomy, and logistics. But longevity interventions need *dense longitudinal data* — not a snapshot every 3 months — to detect biological age changes. Wearables generate this data continuously but aren't validated for clinical endpoints, CROs don't have pipelines for continuous sensor data, and IRBs don't know how to handle it. The Nature consensus paper explicitly flagged wearable-derived digital biomarkers as "considerably promising but relatively understudied" — this is an acknowledged gap with $8-9B of trials needing to run this year.

**Why now:**
FDA issued guidance on digital health technologies (DHTs) in clinical trials in 2025. Wearable hardware accuracy for HRV, sleep architecture, skin temperature (inflammation proxy), gait, and VO2max estimates is now clinically defensible. The longevity field just agreed on 14 consensus biomarkers, several of which wearables can proxy. And trial sponsors have budget pressure — $50K/patient is unsustainable at scale.

**Mechanism:**
A CRO-facing platform: validated wearable protocol bundles (Apple Watch Ultra 2, WHOOP, Garmin Forerunner certified models) plus automated biomarker extraction pipelines that output continuous HRV trends, sleep stage architecture, skin temperature variance, estimated VO2max progression, and gait variability. Sold as an add-on arm to existing Phase 1/2 longevity trial protocols. Handles participant consent, data provenance chain for regulatory submission, and auto-formats output for FDA DHT submission requirements. Reduces per-patient cost by ~40% by replacing 4 out of 8 in-person site visits.

**First wedge:**
CROs running Phase 1/2 longevity trials for Series A-C biotechs. The CRO is the right entry — they're under margin pressure from sponsors and have direct relationships with 10-20 active longevity trial sponsors at once. Reach them through the Association of Clinical Research Organizations (ACRO) and direct sponsorship at the CRO Leadership Summit.

**What kills it:**
Medidata, IQVIA, or Oracle Health bolts this onto their existing trial platforms — they have the enterprise relationships. Also, FDA validation of wearable biomarkers for longevity endpoints specifically (not just cardiovascular) could take 3-5 more years, compressing the addressable market. CROs are notoriously conservative buyers.

**Weirder variant:**
Consumer n=1 longevity self-trial kit. Same validated wearable protocol sold directly to individuals who want to test their own interventions (fasting protocol, metformin microdosing, NMN supplementation) with statistical rigor. Results feed into a de-identified cohort database — effectively running a distributed observational trial at scale. Charge $200/month, build the most valuable real-world longevity intervention database in existence.

## Where I Might Be Wrong

**Weakest idea:** AgeStack (#1). The assumption is that longevity biotechs will pay for interoperability middleware. But their proprietary aging clocks are often their core IP — they have a structural incentive to keep data siloed and incomparable. The buyers (CROs, academic researchers) exist but may not have budget. More likely this becomes an NIH/NIA-funded open consortium, which makes it unmonetizable.

**Assumption needing verification on Span (#2):** Do concierge medicine doctors actually see enough Function Health / Neko patients to make this urgent, or is the addressable market still thin at ~15,000 concierge docs in the US? The data overload problem is real, but the frequency of longevity-data-carrying patients in a typical practice needs validation before building.

**Key risk on TrialSense (#3):** The CRO sales cycle is 12-18 months minimum. A startup burning cash waiting for a CRO pilot to convert to a paid contract could run out of runway before hitting PMF. The consumer n=1 variant might be a faster path to revenue and data.

**What all three assume:** That the longevity sector's 2026 momentum is durable. If GLP-1 drugs (Lilly's next-gen tirzepatide variants) capture all metabolic longevity attention and funding, niche longevity biotech infrastructure becomes a smaller TAM than it looks today.
