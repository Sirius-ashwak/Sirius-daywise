**The underexplored pain point:**
Everyone's funding agent *security* and *identity* (Oak, Zenity) but the number that should scare every board is this: **78-97% of enterprises have an AI agent pilot running, but only 12-14% ever reach production at scale.** The reason isn't the model — it's that pilots run on clean data with one user, and production is messy data, concurrent users, and edge cases nobody tested for. That gap is a wide-open lane nobody's building the *operational readiness* layer for — only the *security* layer.

## Three ideas

### 1. Gauntlet
**Pitch:** Stress-tests your AI agent against messy real data before it hits production and embarrasses you.

**The problem:** Automation/AI leads at mid-market companies (500-5,000 employees) pilot agents on clean, curated scenarios, then get blindsided in production by concurrency, edge cases, and adversarial inputs — which is exactly why 86%+ of pilots never scale. The person who feels this acutely is whoever put their name on the board deck promising ROI this quarter.

**Why now:** Agent execution infrastructure — forkable sandboxes, traffic replay, shadow environments — matured in 2026 to the point you can safely replay real production-like load without touching live systems. At the same time, boards started asking for a "readiness score" instead of "did you pilot it," after a year of public agent failures.

**Mechanism:** Pulls a company's real historical tickets/transactions (PII stripped), replays them through the candidate agent in a shadow sandbox while injecting concurrency spikes and known edge cases, then produces a scorecard (hallucination rate, bad tool-calls, latency under load, compliance violations) that gates promotion to production the way a test suite gates a code merge.

**First wedge:** Teams about to flip the switch on vendor-built support agents (Sierra, Decagon, Agentforce implementations). Reach them through the systems integrators doing the implementation, positioned as a required pre-launch step, not an optional add-on.

**What kills it:** Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Microsoft bundle readiness-scoring straight into their own agent builders within a year — this becomes a feature of the platform, not a company.

**Weirder variant:** Make the certification portable — a neutral third party issues the agent a "license" (a bar exam for a legal-assist agent, a boards exam for a clinical-documentation agent) that travels with it across employers, so the next hospital hiring that AI scribe checks its license instead of re-testing from scratch.

---

### 2. Punchlist
**Pitch:** Turns foremen's voice memos and job-site photos into reconciled invoices and change orders automatically.

**The problem:** On $5M-$50M construction jobs, reconciling subcontractor invoices against daily logs, photos, and verbal change orders is a manual slog — a project engineer burns 10-15 hours a week just confirming "did this actually happen" before a bill gets approved. One missed change order eats real margin on a job that's already thin.

**Why now:** Vision-language models got cheap and accurate enough in 2026 to reliably parse handwritten daily logs, blurry jobsite photos, and rambling voice memos — the exact messy inputs that made this un-automatable until now. Crews already run this data through whatever jobsite chat app they use; it's just never been read.

**Mechanism:** The agent ingests the foreman's existing voice notes and photos from whatever app the crew already uses (no new app to adopt), cross-references them against subcontract scope and prior change orders, flags discrepancies before an invoice is approved, and auto-drafts the change-order paperwork.

**First wedge:** The messy v1 is one overworked project engineer at a GC doing this by hand in spreadsheets. Skip construction-tech marketing (slow, relationship-driven) — sell into the construction bookkeeping/back-office firms that already do this reconciliation as a service, as a labor multiplier for them.

**What kills it:** Liability. If the agent misreads a change order and a sub gets underpaid or a GC gets overbilled, that's a contract dispute with real money attached — one bad miss kills trust overnight in an industry with near-zero tolerance for AI mistakes on money.

**Weirder variant:** Generalize the reconciliation engine into a neutral AI arbitrator — both sides of any messy B2B billing dispute (GC vs. sub, freight broker vs. carrier, staffing agency vs. client) feed in their version of events and it renders a preliminary settlement recommendation. A dispute-resolution layer that sits above both parties, not a tool either side buys alone.

### 3. Roster
**Pitch:** An HRIS for your company's AI agents — deployment, cost, and offboarding in one place.

**The problem:** Mid-size enterprises now run 20-50 point agents from a dozen vendors — a sales agent here, a support agent there, a finance-reconciliation agent from someone else — and nobody has a single system of record for which agents exist, what access they hold, what they cost per action, and what happens when a vendor contract lapses. The ops/IT leader inherits this mess and finds out during a security audit or a surprise token bill.

**Why now:** 2026 is the year agent sprawl became real — departments self-serve agent tools the way they used to self-serve SaaS seats, except agents take actions and cost money per call. The non-human-identity wave (Oak, Zenity) proved companies will pay to see their agent fleet, but that wave sells security — the operational/HR side (lifecycle, cost, performance) is still wide open.

**Mechanism:** Connects to the identity provider and major agent platforms via API to auto-discover every agent with production access, builds a "personnel file" per agent (who deployed it, what it can touch, cost per action, error/override rate over time), and runs a lightweight review cycle flagging agents that have gone stale or now cost more than the labor they replaced.

**First wedge:** The messy v1 is the IT/ops lead who just got burned — a runaway token bill or an audit finding a zombie agent with live access. Reach them through the same audit/compliance consultants who already sell into that panic moment.

**What kills it:** This is a feature, not a company. Oak or Zenity (or Okta/Workday) bolt a lifecycle-and-cost dashboard onto their existing agent-identity platform within a year, since the hard part — agent discovery — is already theirs.

**Weirder variant:** Push past cost-tracking into actual "agent compensation" — agents get budget/autonomy raises or demotions based on measured performance, the way a manager adjusts a report's scope after a review. The review cycle becomes an automated incentive/guardrail-tuning loop, not just a dashboard.

## Where I might be wrong

**Roster** is the weakest of the three — I basically talked myself into its own failure mode ("this is a feature, not a company"), and Oak/Zenity have $60-125M and an obvious incentive to ship a lifecycle dashboard within a quarter. The whole idea rests on lifecycle/cost management being owned by a *different* buyer than agent security (FinOps-style cost owner vs. security team) — if it's the same budget line, this folds into the governance players immediately and there's no standalone company here. **Punchlist**'s liability concern also cuts both ways: if I de-risk it by making the agent "flag for human review" instead of "auto-approve" to dodge the lawsuit risk, I also cut the time savings that make it worth buying in the first place — that tension needs testing with an actual GC, not assumed away.
