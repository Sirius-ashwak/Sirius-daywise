## Three ideas

### 1. Rosterly — an HRIS, but the employees are your AI agents

**Pitch:** Workday for your AI agents — hire, badge, review, and fire them like staff.

**The problem:** Companies running Ema, Sierra, Glean, or homegrown LangGraph agents now have dozens of agents touching real systems — approving expenses, emailing customers, filing tickets — with no single record of which agent did what, under whose authorization, at what cost, or when it should be retired. IT ops and compliance teams find out an agent is still running six months after the project that spawned it ended.

**Why now:** Ema's raise (and its total $140M) is proof enterprises are deploying agent *teams*, not single bots, this year. Once you have a team, you need a roster. Non-human identity is also becoming its own security category, but nobody's built the HR layer — onboarding, performance review, offboarding — on top of the identity layer.

**Mechanism:** Every agent gets a "badge" (identity + scoped permissions + cost center) at creation. Rosterly ingests logs from whatever agent platforms and orchestration frameworks a company runs, builds a per-agent activity and cost timeline, flags agents that are stale, over-permissioned, or duplicating another agent's job, and produces a plain-English "performance review" a manager actually reads before a renewal or expansion decision.

**First wedge:** Mid-market companies that are already power users of one agent platform (Ema customers, Sierra customers) and are starting to sprawl into 5-10 agents. Reach them through the agent platforms' own partner/integration programs — "connect Rosterly to see your agent roster" is a natural post-onboarding upsell nobody's offering yet.

**What kills it:** Microsoft (Entra Agent ID), Okta, or the agent platforms themselves (Ema, Sierra) bundle this natively as a dashboard feature before Rosterly gets distribution — this is the single most obvious "the platform eats the feature" risk in the whole idea.

**Weirder variant:** Same registry-and-review model, pointed at school districts tracking AI tutoring/grading agents teachers deploy in their own classrooms — a "substitute roster" for bots, with the school board as the audience instead of the CFO.

### 2. Counterparty — an agent that negotiates your SaaS and AI renewals against the vendor's own sales agent

**Pitch:** Your AI negotiates your software renewals against their AI, so you don't have to.

**The problem:** Procurement now decides 53% of B2B buying cycles and is drowning — a midsized company runs 100+ SaaS tools plus a "growing pile of AI subscriptions purchased outside any formal process." Renewal negotiations are manual, repetitive, and increasingly one-sided because vendors are already putting AI SDRs and renewal agents on their side of the table.

**Why now:** Vendor-side sales is rapidly agentifying (AI SDRs doing outbound, AI account managers handling renewals) — buyers are about to be out-negotiated by machines unless they field their own. Agent-to-agent commerce protocols are also maturing enough this year that two parties' agents can actually transact rather than just chat.

**Mechanism:** Counterparty ingests a company's current contract terms, usage data, and market comps, then negotiates directly with the vendor's sales agent (or a human, structured the same way) — proposing terms, trading concessions, and escalating to a human on either side only when it hits a configured floor/ceiling. Every exchange is logged and auditable.

**First wedge:** Mid-market finance/procurement teams heading into a renewal-heavy quarter, sold as "get renegotiated quotes on your top 10 renewals for free" — low-commitment, immediate ROI, easy to viral through CFO/procurement Slack communities.

**What kills it:** Vendors simply refuse to let an unknown counterparty agent transact (anti-bot walls on their side), or buyers balk at letting an agent commit to legally binding terms without a human in the loop — this is the most speculative of the three on both the tech and the trust front.

**Weirder variant:** Point the same agent-vs-agent negotiation engine at consumer retail — a personal shopping agent that haggles in real time against a retailer's dynamic-pricing agent, bringing negotiated pricing back to online consumer purchases.

### 3. Probe — continuous AI-vendor risk certification for regulated industries

**Pitch:** Vanta did compliance checklists; Probe actually attacks your AI vendors' models, continuously.

**The problem:** Procurement teams in healthcare, legal, and finance now have to answer questions a standard SOC 2 was never built for: does this vendor's model train on our data, can it be prompt-injected, where does the data actually reside. Today that's a static questionnaire answered once at signing, unmonitored between renewals — model behavior can drift or get patched (badly) at any point in between.

**Why now:** Procurement's diligence checklist changed this year to explicitly include AI governance, training opt-outs, and prompt-injection defense — but no continuous mechanism exists to verify vendor claims after the ink dries, only point-in-time attestations.

**Mechanism:** Probe runs scheduled, sandboxed red-team probes against a vendor's live AI product (prompt injection attempts, data-leakage tests, training-opt-out verification) and issues a continuously-updated risk score procurement teams can pull into their own vendor review, rather than a PDF from six months ago.

**First wedge:** Compliance officers at mid-market healthcare and legal orgs who already have to manually vet every new AI vendor and have no good tooling for the AI-specific questions — reach through healthcare IT compliance communities and legal-ops conferences, positioned as a faster, cheaper alternative to their current manual vendor-security review.

**What kills it:** Vendors refuse continuous external probing of their production systems (understandable security posture), or existing GRC platforms (Vanta, Drata) bundle a shallow version of this as a feature before Probe can differentiate on depth.

**Weirder variant:** Same continuous-verification engine pointed at dating apps and marketplaces — ongoing certification that a profile's photos/bio weren't AI-generated or that a marketplace listing's "verified" badge still holds up under re-probing, not just at signup.

## Where I might be wrong

**Counterparty** is the weakest of the three — it assumes vendors will let an unfamiliar buy-side agent negotiate against their sales agent at all, and assumes buyers will trust an autonomous agent to commit to contract terms without a human final check. Both are live open questions, not settled trends yet; this one needs a "will a real vendor's sales team actually engage with this" test before anything else. **Rosterly** is the safest bet — it's riding a very real, already-funded trend (agent sprawl inside real companies) — but its biggest risk is platform bundling, so the wedge needs to move fast into companies running *multiple* agent platforms at once, where no single platform vendor has the full picture and therefore no single vendor is incentivized to build the cross-platform roster themselves.
