## Three ideas

### 1. Ledger Leash
**One-line pitch:** A policy firewall that sits between AI agents and every payment rail they touch, enforcing limits and logging everything.

**The specific problem:** Finance and eng teams are being told to give autonomous agents a corporate card, ACH access, or a stablecoin wallet — and there's no standard way to say "this agent can spend up to $500/day on SaaS renewals, never on payroll, and here's an audit trail a controller can actually read." Today that's duct-taped together with API keys and hope.

**Why now:** 2026 is the year agents got write access to money (procurement bots, AI travel bookers, autonomous trading assistants), but the payment rails themselves — cards, ACH, stablecoin — were built for humans clicking "confirm," not for a policy engine mediating machine-initiated spend at scale.

**Mechanism:** A middleware layer issues each agent a scoped, revocable "financial identity" (virtual card, ACH sub-account, or wallet) tied to a declarative policy (spend caps, categories, time windows, kill switch). Every transaction routes through Ledger Leash first, which approves/denies against policy in real time and writes an immutable, human-readable audit log — regardless of which underlying rail (Visa, ACH, stablecoin) actually settles it.

**First wedge:** Mid-market finance ops teams rolling out their first agentic procurement or expense-approval bot — sell to the controller who's terrified of the "what if it just buys something" scenario, not to the AI team.

**What kills it:** This is a rail, and rails have a chicken-and-egg problem — card networks, banks, and stablecoin issuers all want to own the policy layer themselves (Stripe, Circle, and issuing-focused agent startups are already circling this exact space).

**Weirder variant:** Consumer version — parents issue their kid's AI tutor or shopping assistant a scoped "allowance identity" with the same spend-cap-and-kill-switch mechanics, marketed as the digital version of a debit card with parental controls.

---

### 2. RailSwitch
**One-line pitch:** One payout API that silently arbitrages between fiat and stablecoin rails per-transaction for cost and speed.

**The specific problem:** Vertical SaaS platforms doing cross-border payouts (gig marketplaces, creator platforms, freelance networks) are stuck choosing one rail — usually a single embedded-finance provider — and eating whatever fees and settlement delays that rail has for a given corridor, even when a cheaper or faster route exists transaction-by-transaction.

**Why now:** Stablecoin settlement just became genuinely competitive with fiat rails on cost and speed in most corridors (Tempo crossed $1B in 30-day stablecoin transfer volume; three major stablecoin neobanks launched in the last two months), but no one's built the arbitrage layer that picks the best rail per payout automatically — every player (Airwallex, TabaPay, Fasset) is still selling you their one rail.

**Mechanism:** Platforms integrate one API for payouts; RailSwitch holds relationships with multiple fiat and stablecoin rails per corridor and routes each payout to whichever is cheapest/fastest given the recipient's country, amount, and urgency, converting behind the scenes so the recipient just sees money land. Platforms get one reconciliation feed regardless of which rail actually moved the funds.

**First wedge:** Emerging-market gig/creator payout platforms already frustrated by FX spread and settlement lag on their current embedded-finance provider — undercut on cost for a narrow set of high-volume corridors first, then expand.

**What kills it:** Regulatory licensing is per-rail and per-corridor (money transmission, stablecoin issuance oversight from MAS-style regulators), so the "arbitrage" promise gets throttled by how many jurisdictions you can actually get licensed in before a bigger player like Airwallex just adds stablecoin routing to their existing platform.

**Weirder variant:** Turn it inward — sell RailSwitch's routing engine as embedded infrastructure *to* the embedded-finance platforms themselves (Airwallex, Fasset, Ripple/SettleMint), so instead of competing with them you become the routing layer underneath all of them.

---

### 3. Robo-Claims
**One-line pitch:** Dispute resolution and insurance built specifically for purchases an AI agent made wrong.

**The specific problem:** Enterprise procurement agents and consumer shopping assistants are starting to make real purchase mistakes — wrong SKU, over-authorized quantity, a bad price negotiated against a scummy counterparty agent — and the existing chargeback/dispute machinery (issuer vs. acquirer vs. card network) was never designed for machine-initiated errors happening at machine speed and volume.

**Why now:** Agentic commerce crossed from demo to deployment in 2026 (IMF and industry commentary are now treating "agent negotiates and pays" as a 2026 phenomenon, not a future one), and the first wave of real disputes is starting to hit companies with no playbook for who's liable — the agent vendor, the platform, or the buyer.

**Mechanism:** Robo-Claims sits alongside a company's procurement/shopping agents, logging each agent-initiated transaction's decision trail (what it was authorized to do, what it actually did, why). When something goes wrong, that trail feeds a fast-track arbitration process plus an insurance product that pays out for agent-error losses above a deductible — effectively transaction insurance underwritten on machine-readable intent logs instead of human dispute forms.

**First wedge:** Companies piloting AI procurement agents for indirect spend (office supplies, SaaS renewals) who need a way to tell their CFO "yes we have a safety net" before they'll expand the pilot — sell through the same finance ops buyer as Ledger Leash, as a bundled add-on.

**What kills it:** Insurance is capital-intensive and slow to underwrite from scratch; this is more likely to succeed as a feature an existing commercial insurer or card network bolts on than as a standalone startup, unless it can move fast enough to become the de facto data standard before they do.

**Weirder variant:** A public, crowdsourced "agent reputation ledger" — every agent's dispute history becomes a portable trust score other platforms can check before letting that agent transact with them, like a credit score for AI agents.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Robo-Claims is the weakest of the three.** Insurance products need real actuarial data and regulatory capital before anyone will underwrite them, and "AI agent makes a purchase mistake" doesn't have enough loss history yet to price — this might really be a data-collection play (build the decision-trail logging first, sell the insurance product in 18 months) rather than a fundable startup today. **Ledger Leash** is also crowded territory — I should verify how differentiated it actually is from what Stripe's and Circle's agent-toolkit efforts are already shipping, since "give an agent a scoped financial identity" is exactly the pitch several better-capitalized incumbents are already making. **RailSwitch** rests on the assumption that stablecoin rails stay cost-competitive once regulatory clarity (or lack of it — see the G20/FSB stablecoin review still pending) settles; if regulation tightens the stablecoin side specifically, the arbitrage math could evaporate fast.
