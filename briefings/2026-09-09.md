**The gap everyone's arguing about:**
Power transformers now carry ~128-week lead times, generator step-up units ~144 weeks, and switchgear is sold out through 2028. Interconnection queues hold 2+ terawatts of requested capacity and average five years to clear. Result: up to half of the 16GW of data center capacity slated for 2026 is slipping to 2027+, and the largest developers are responding by going around the grid entirely — building their own gas turbines and onsite generation. [Geopolitical Economist](https://medium.com/the-geopolitical-economist/everyone-is-watching-the-ai-boom-nobody-is-watching-the-transformer-that-cant-be-built-in-time-341c523e8e81), [ChargedUpPro](https://chargeduppro.com/post/data-center-transformer-shortage-power-bottleneck-industrial-property-2026), [CFR](https://www.cfr.org/articles/us-interconnection-challenge-why-renewables-are-stuck-line)

Nearly everyone funded this cycle is attacking the demand side (software to shave load) or the generation side (nuclear, gas, thermal batteries). Almost nobody is attacking the *equipment* bottleneck itself, and almost nobody is helping developers figure out *where* the queue is actually short before they commit to a site.

## Three ideas

### 1. Salvage Grid
**Pitch:** Buy the transformers utilities are scrapping, remanufacture them, sell 18-month lead time as a 6-week one.

**The problem:** A data center developer with a signed interconnection agreement still can't get power because the transformer they need has a 128-week factory lead time. Meanwhile, utilities are decommissioning thousands of serviceable transformers and switchgear units every year as coal plants retire and substations get upgraded — most of that equipment gets scrapped for copper, not resold, because there's no trusted channel to recondition and recertify it.

**Why now:** Two curves are crossing in 2026 for the first time — record fossil plant retirements (freeing up used gear) and record new transformer demand (data centers), while OEM capacity is sold out for years. That crossing didn't exist five years ago; retirements were slower and data center demand was a fraction of this.

**Mechanism:** A brokerage + remanufacturing operation that buys decommissioned transformers and switchgear from utilities and industrial sites before they hit the scrap yard, runs them through a standardized refurb-and-recertify process (oil replacement, winding tests, bushing swaps), and sells them with a warranty to developers who need power in months, not years. Revenue is the spread between scrap price and remanufactured price, which is enormous given OEM scarcity pricing.

**First wedge:** Mid-size data center developers and colo operators (not hyperscalers, who can just wait or self-generate) who are stuck mid-project with a signed interconnection agreement and no transformer. Reach them through the same EPC/engineering firms already scouting for equipment on gray-market broker forums.

**What kills it:** Utilities may refuse to sell decommissioned gear to third parties for liability reasons, or OEMs could gate recertification/warranty support to protect new-unit sales, leaving no clean legal path to a bankable warranty.

**Weirder variant:** Do the same thing for EV fast-charging sites — remanufactured substation-grade transformers sized down for highway charging plazas, which face an identical queue problem at a smaller scale.

### 2. QueueScore
**Pitch:** Predicts how many years a proposed site will actually wait for power, before you buy the land.

**The problem:** Data center and renewable developers pick sites based on land cost, fiber, and water — then find out 18 months into permitting that their substation is 4th in a queue with 2 years of study backlog ahead of it. The queue data (FERC, ISO filings, utility interconnection studies) is public but scattered across thousands of PDFs and portals with no standardized scoring.

**Why now:** ISOs digitized and started publishing structured interconnection queue data over the last two years specifically because of the AI-driven backlog political pressure — that machine-readable substrate didn't reliably exist before 2025. At the same time, the cost of guessing wrong just went from "annoying delay" to "your $2B facility sits idle."

**Mechanism:** Scrape and normalize interconnection queue filings, historical study timelines, and transformer/switchgear procurement data across every US ISO and major utility, then train a model on completed projects to predict time-to-energization for any candidate substation or queue position. Sell it as a site-selection SaaS to data center developers, renewable IPPs, and the real estate brokers who serve them, priced per site evaluated.

**First wedge:** Boutique data center site-selection consultants and second-tier developers who can't afford an in-house grid-strategy team the way hyperscalers can. Reach them through the same trade conferences (DatacenterDynamics, Data Center World) where site selectors already gather.

**What kills it:** Utilities and ISOs could simply be too inconsistent or opaque in their filings for a model to generalize, or a big real estate brokerage (JLL, CBRE) builds the same thing in-house and gives it away as a loss leader to win site-selection mandates.

**Weirder variant:** Turn it inside out and sell it to utilities themselves — a tool that lets them show developers a live, credible "time to power" number instead of a form letter, turning queue transparency into a competitive advantage for utilities trying to attract data center tax revenue.

### 3. Sidestream
**Pitch:** A matching engine that pairs data center waste heat and water with the farm, greenhouse, or district heat loop next door.

**The problem:** A hyperscale data center dumps enough waste heat to warm a small town, and (per WAVR) enough atmospheric water is recoverable from its cooling exhaust to matter for nearby agriculture — but almost none of it is used, because the data center operator has no incentive or expertise to go find a heat/water buyer, and the greenhouse or district heat operator next door has no idea the resource exists or how to contract for it.

**Why now:** The sheer number of new hyperscale sites breaking ground in 2026, often in exurban and rural areas near cheap land and power, puts them physically adjacent to agriculture and small municipal heat networks for the first time at scale — this is a siting pattern that's new to this cycle, not a five-year-old opportunity nobody noticed.

**Mechanism:** A marketplace and deal-structuring platform that identifies data centers under construction, models their heat/water output, finds compatible nearby users (greenhouses, aquaculture, district heating utilities, industrial dryers), and packages standardized offtake contracts plus the pipe/heat-exchanger engineering needed to connect them — taking a cut of the offtake contract value.

**First wedge:** Greenhouse and vertical farm operators near announced data center builds, who are desperate to cut heating costs and are far easier to sign than a cautious data center operator — sign the demand side first, then use that signed offtake to pitch the data center as "we already have your buyer."

**What kills it:** Data center operators may see any physical interconnection to a third party as an unacceptable operational risk or liability, killing deals regardless of how attractive the economics look on paper.

**Weirder variant:** Apply the same matching logic to CO2 instead of heat — data centers' backup generators and any onsite gas turbines produce a CO2 stream that could feed adjacent greenhouse CO2 enrichment systems, a second symbiotic resource stream riding the same relationship.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Sidestream** is the weakest. It depends on data center operators accepting physical, contractual entanglement with an outside party at their most sensitive piece of infrastructure — cooling and power — and every signal from the industry this year (Emerald AI's software-only approach, the self-generation trend) points toward operators wanting *less* external dependency, not more. The wedge of "sign the greenhouse first" helps, but the core assumption — that a data center operator will sign a 10+ year offtake contract with a farm — needs a real conversation with a data center facilities team before it's worth building anything. **Salvage Grid** rests on an easier-to-verify assumption (utilities' willingness to sell decommissioned equipment) that a few phone calls to utility asset-recovery departments could confirm or kill in a week.
