## Three ideas

### 1. Counterweight
**Pitch:** Impact-modeling and negotiation software for the towns being asked to approve the next data center.

**The problem:** A county planning board with three part-time members and no engineering staff gets a rezoning application from a hyperscaler's lawyers, backed by a paid consultant's impact study. They have two weeks to respond and no way to verify the numbers on power draw, water use, or tax abatement terms.

**Why now:** This is the first year the opposition has real legislative hooks (moratoriums, the Ratepayer Protection Act) to act on — but only if local boards can actually engage on the substance instead of just voting no or yes blind. The data needed to do this (utility tariff filings, interconnection queue data, comparable PILOT deals) has only recently become machine-readable at scale.

**Mechanism:** Feed in a project's public filings (site plan, interconnection application, proposed PILOT/tax deal) and Counterweight cross-references a database of comparable data center deals nationwide, flags terms that are worse than the median, and models real water/power draw against the local grid's actual headroom — output is a plain-language memo a planning board can put on the record.

**First wedge:** Give it free to the ~50 county planning departments in the hottest corridors (Virginia, Georgia, Ohio, Texas) and to the grassroots opposition networks already organizing in 49 states — they need ammunition today and will spread it themselves. Monetize later by selling the comparable-deals database to the utilities and developers who want to know what terms will actually clear.

**What kills it:** Gets captured as a purely anti-development tool, loses credibility with the boards that actually want the jobs and tax base but better terms — becomes "the NIMBY app" instead of a neutral diligence tool.

**Weirder variant:** Turn it into a public leaderboard ranking every proposed U.S. data center by "community cost per job created" — a viral scorecard site, not enterprise software.

### 2. Ratepayer Docket
**Pitch:** Parses utility rate filings and auto-drafts ratepayer interventions against data-center-driven bill hikes.

**The problem:** State Public Utility Commissions are being flooded with new tariff filings for "large load" data center customers, and the people meant to fight for households (understaffed state ratepayer advocate offices, local reporters) can't read hundreds of pages of filings per docket fast enough to intervene before comment deadlines close.

**Why now:** The Ratepayer Protection Act passing the House 417-3 on Sept 16 creates, for the first time, a formal mandate for states to scrutinize this exact cost-shifting — meaning a new category of PUC docket is about to open up nationwide that didn't functionally exist eighteen months ago.

**Mechanism:** An LLM pipeline ingests PUC tariff filings and interconnection cost-allocation agreements, flags the specific clauses that shift data-center infrastructure cost onto residential rate classes, and generates a ready-to-file intervention comment with citations — cutting a multi-week legal review down to an afternoon.

**First wedge:** Give the tool free to state ratepayer advocate offices and the local journalists already covering these fights (they're the ones with public credibility and standing); charge intervenor coalitions and consumer law firms for the filing-automation tier.

**What kills it:** Utilities and data center developers have every incentive to keep filings opaque and to lobby state legislatures to preempt or narrow ratepayer standing — the tool's entire value depends on a regulatory door that could get closed by the same political fight it's riding.

**Weirder variant:** Generalize past electricity to water utility filings, since data centers' water draw is becoming the second front of the same fight.

### 3. Queue Clearinghouse
**Pitch:** A matchmaking market pairing stalled interconnection queue positions with developers who need speed now.

**The problem:** ~8,200 projects are sitting in U.S. interconnection queues (1,312 GW of generation, 749 GW of storage); many will withdraw after sinking years and real money into studies, while data center developers desperate for firm power would pay well for a queue position that's already been studied.

**Why now:** Automated interconnection study software (SUGAR cutting study time 98%, PJM's HyperQ validating site control) has, only this year, turned queue and study data into something machine-checkable and comparable at scale — the precondition for any real-time matching market, rather than a manual, relationship-driven broker process.

**Mechanism:** The platform ingests public and utility-shared queue data, scores stalled or at-risk positions by how much study work is already sunk and how transferable the position is under the relevant ISO's rules, and connects the original developer with a buyer looking to skip years of queue time — collecting a placement fee on completed transfers.

**First wedge:** Start manually — a newsletter and direct outreach to independent solar/storage/geothermal developers (like Mazama) sitting on stalled positions, brokering a handful of deals by hand before building the marketplace software.

**What kills it:** Most ISOs restrict interconnection rights from being assigned or sold without a fresh interconnection agreement — the entire premise could be legally dead on arrival unless specific ISOs explicitly permit transfer, which needs verifying before writing another line of code.

**Weirder variant:** Apply the same clearinghouse mechanic to water rights and permits, the other resource now gating data center siting.

---

## Where I might be wrong

**Queue Clearinghouse is the weakest of the three** — I haven't verified that any major ISO (PJM, MISO, ERCOT, CAISO) actually permits interconnection queue positions to be sold or assigned to a new owner; most tariffs I'm aware of require a fresh interconnection study when project ownership or specs change materially, which could make the core transaction illegal or pointless as designed. That needs a real regulatory read before this is fundable. Counterweight and Ratepayer Docket both depend on an assumption that local boards and ratepayer advocates *want* better tooling rather than political cover to just say no or yes on gut instinct — worth testing with a couple of real planning board members before building anything.
