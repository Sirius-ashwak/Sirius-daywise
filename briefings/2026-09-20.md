## The three ideas

### 1. Flightbox
**One-line pitch:** A cryptographically signed black-box recorder for AI agents, sold to insurers writing their liability policies.

**The problem:** Companies deploying agents that touch money, health data, or customer commitments have no admissible, tamper-proof record of *why* an agent did what it did — and insurers won't underwrite agent liability without one. Right now "documented governance" means a screenshot of a dashboard someone can edit after the fact.

**Why now:** EU AI Act logging is now enforceable, Colorado/Texas AI Acts are live, and E&O insurers are starting to write (and price) agent-specific liability riders — but they have no standardized evidence format to underwrite against, the same gap cyber insurance hit in 2015 before SOC 2 became the de facto proof-of-controls.

**Mechanism:** An SDK vertical SaaS vendors and enterprises drop into their agent runtime that hashes every agent decision (inputs, model version, tool calls, outputs) into an append-only, notarized ledger. Insurers and auditors get a query API instead of a PDF; premiums get priced off the ledger's completeness score instead of a self-attestation questionnaire.

**First wedge:** Mid-market fintechs and healthtechs who just got a Colorado/Texas AI Act compliance letter from a customer's legal team and need something to hand back this quarter, not after a 9-month SOC 2 cycle. Reach them through the compliance consultants and fractional CISOs who are already fielding these letters.

**What kills it:** Insurers standardize on their own in-house logging requirement (or a Lloyd's-style consortium spec) instead of adopting a third-party format, cutting Flightbox out of the loop it was built to serve.

**Weirder variant:** Same black box, but for individual consumers' personal agents — a "flight recorder" people can subpoena their own AI assistant's decision log from, submitted as evidence in a small-claims dispute with a landlord, an airline, or an insurer who used an agent against them.

### 2. Ridealong
**One-line pitch:** Compliance-in-a-box that lets a vertical SaaS vendor ship its first AI agent feature without hiring a security team.

**The problem:** A 40-person vertical SaaS company (practice management, claims software, trucking dispatch — pick a niche) wants to ship an agent feature to compete with Agentforce-style entrants, but their two-person eng team can't build SOC 2-grade logging, non-human identity scoping, and EU/Colorado/Texas-compliant audit trails from scratch, and a $20-50K external audit before they even have paying agent users is a non-starter.

**Why now:** Gartner's 40%-of-enterprise-apps-have-agents-by-end-of-2026 number is a race every vertical SaaS incumbent now feels, but the governance tooling (Boomi, Zenity) is being built for enterprises with security teams — not for the long tail of vertical ISVs who are the ones actually under competitive pressure to ship an agent this quarter.

**Mechanism:** A drop-in middleware layer that sits between a vertical SaaS vendor's new agent feature and their existing product, auto-generating scoped non-human identities per agent action, logging in the format regulators and enterprise procurement teams expect, and producing a pre-filled compliance questionnaire the vendor can hand to its own enterprise customers' security reviewers.

**First wedge:** Vertical SaaS ISVs with $2-10M ARR in a regulated niche (healthcare ops, insurance claims, trucking/logistics) who are about to ship their first agent feature and are getting blocked in enterprise sales cycles by "show us your AI governance." Reach them through the vertical SaaS Slack communities and via the security-review consultants who keep seeing the same gap across their ISV clients.

**What kills it:** The big vertical SaaS platforms (Salesforce, ServiceTitan, Procore) commoditize this as a built-in feature of their own agent-building tools, making Ridealong redundant for anyone building on top of them rather than from scratch.

**Weirder variant:** Same middleware, aimed at city and county governments deploying agents for permitting and benefits eligibility — output is a public-facing, FOIA-able "agent personnel file" citizens can actually query, not just an internal audit log.

### 3. Roster
**One-line pitch:** The HRIS for non-human employees — onboarding, review, and firing agents the way you manage people.

**The problem:** Enterprises are now naming their agents (Casey, Paige, Carter) and giving them job functions, but they're managing that headcount with spreadsheets and Slack threads — there's no system of record for which agent has which permissions, how it's performing, who approved its last capability upgrade, or how it gets safely decommissioned when it starts misbehaving.

**Why now:** Non-human identities already outnumber humans roughly 50-to-1 inside the average enterprise, and Salesforce just normalized treating agents as named staff with job titles rather than app features — the anthropomorphization that makes "HR for agents" make intuitive sense to a buyer wasn't true a year ago.

**Mechanism:** A system of record that treats each deployed agent like an employee record: an onboarding flow that provisions scoped access, a performance dashboard tracking task success/override rates in place of a review cycle, and an offboarding flow that revokes credentials and archives its action history — all surfaced in language (and workflows) an HR or IT ops person, not just a security engineer, can operate.

**First wedge:** Mid-size enterprises (2,000-10,000 employees) that have deployed 10+ agents across departments without a central register and just had a "wait, which team owns the agent that emailed that customer?" incident. Reach them through IT ops and People-ops leaders, not security — this is a category-naming play, sold as "the Workday gap" rather than a security tool.

**What kills it:** IT asset management incumbents (ServiceNow, Workday itself) add an "agent record type" to their existing platform, and buyers default to extending a tool they already pay for instead of adopting a new system of record.

**Weirder variant:** Same product, but for a gig-economy of agents a company doesn't own — a "temp agency for agents" where you rent a named, insured, performance-tracked agent from a marketplace for a single task, with Roster as the staffing record connecting the temp agent to the hiring company's systems.

## Where I might be wrong

Roster is the weakest of the three — "HRIS for agents" is a clean narrative but it's not obvious HR or IT-ops buyers, rather than security/platform teams, actually hold budget for this, and it risks being a vitamin (nice framing) rather than a painkiller (Flightbox and Ridealong both have a forcing function — an insurer or an enterprise procurement blocker — that makes someone pay this quarter). The assumption most worth stress-testing across all three: that "AI agent governance" stays a standalone budget line rather than getting absorbed into whichever big platform (Salesforce, ServiceNow, Microsoft) wins the underlying agent-runtime war and bundles governance in for free — if that consolidation happens fast, all three ideas need to be infrastructure a platform *wants* to integrate rather than a tool that competes with it.
