## Three ideas

### 1. ProtocolAudit
**Pitch:** Feed it your labs and your supplement stack; it tells you which ones are statistically doing anything.

**Problem:** People spending $350–$150,000/year on longevity protocols (NAD+, rapamycin, peptides, cold plunge, you name it) have zero way to know if *their* numbers are actually moving because of the intervention, confounders, or noise. Acute for the "quantified self" crowd already paying for panels but flying blind on causality.

**Why now:** Function Health and Lifeforce alone have put structured, repeated biomarker panels into 50,000+ people's hands for the first time — that's the longitudinal n needed to run real within-person statistics. Three years ago this data didn't exist at consumer scale.

**Mechanism:** User connects their lab-panel exports (Function, Lifeforce, InsideTracker) and logs their protocol calendar. A time-series model treats each supplement/intervention start date as a natural experiment against their own biomarker trend line, controls for known confounders (sleep, training load pulled from wearables), and outputs a confidence-scored verdict per intervention — "your CRP dropped 40% starting 3 weeks after you added X; here's the confidence interval."

**First wedge:** Biohacker subreddits and longevity Substacks (the same crowd already tracking this in spreadsheets) — sell it as an add-on report that plugs into a Function/Lifeforce export, not a new panel.

**What kills it:** Most biomarkers move too slowly and too noisily for n=1 causal inference to ever reach real significance — you could ship a product that gives false confidence dressed up as rigor, which is worse than the status quo.

**Weirder variant:** Sell it to self-insured employers as a wellness-benefit auditor — run it across the anonymized aggregate of employees using a given perk (standing desks, GLP-1 coverage, mental health app) and kill the ones with no measurable effect on claims costs.

### 2. WetLab Ledger
**Pitch:** A cryptographically-attested wet-lab verification network for the flood of AI-generated drug candidates nobody can validate fast enough.

**Problem:** AI drug-discovery money is now moving faster than wet-lab throughput — $2.64B went into the category between March and July 2026 alone. Every funded AI-biotech is generating candidate molecules faster than any single lab can run assays, and acute for the *not*-mega-funded ones (seed/Series A) who can't afford to build in-house wet labs like Earendil or Iambic can.

**Why now:** The AI-biotech funding surge (Takeda-Iambic's $1.7B deal, Earendil's $787M round, two IPOs in one month) has made "we designed 10,000 candidates" a commodity claim — the differentiator is now who can actually prove which ones work, and prove it in a way a pharma partner or acquirer will trust without redoing the work themselves.

**Mechanism:** Startups submit candidate molecules through an API; the network routes assays to a marketplace of accredited CRO wet labs, and every result — from sample prep to raw instrument output — gets hashed and timestamped on a shared ledger before any human touches the data, so results can't be quietly re-run until they look good. Buyers (pharma BD teams, acquirers, later-stage investors) can verify a candidate's full assay history without trusting the startup's own PowerPoint.

**First wedge:** The long tail of AI-biotech seed and Series A companies who need third-party-credible validation to raise their next round or land a pharma partnership, reached through biotech accelerators (IndieBio-style) and CRO networks hungry for AI-native customers.

**What kills it:** The mega-funded players who actually set the market narrative (Earendil, Iambic) are vertically integrating their own wet labs — if the marketplace only serves companies too small to matter, it's a niche compliance tool, not a category.

**Weirder variant:** Point the same trust-ledger infrastructure at agricultural biotech — AI-generated crop traits face the identical model-output-vs-field-trial-capacity bottleneck, with far less regulatory drag than pharma.

### 3. Signal Notary
**Pitch:** A trust layer that turns raw wearable streams into clinically admissible, liability-safe data doctors will actually act on.

**Problem:** 2026's wearables (Adaptyx's cortisol sensor, cuffless BP rings, Oura's AI-physician front door) generate clinical-grade *signal*, but physicians and insurers still won't act on consumer device data because there's no chain of custody — no way to prove the data wasn't spoofed, gamed, or corrupted before a treatment or underwriting decision gets made on it. Acute for longevity clinics (like the ones running on Longevitix) that want to fold wearable data into physician-ready summaries but carry the liability if it's wrong.

**Why now:** Edge AI chips now let wearables run diagnostic-grade inference on-device, and device-attestation hardware (secure enclaves, TPM-style chips) has become cheap enough to put in consumer wearables — the pieces to cryptographically prove "this reading came from this unmodified sensor, unaltered" only became commodity hardware this year.

**Mechanism:** An SDK wearable makers embed that signs each data point at the point of capture using the device's secure enclave; a middleware layer aggregates signed streams from multiple devices, runs an on-device/edge model to flag anomalies or tampering, and outputs a single attested summary formatted to slot into an EHR or underwriting decision — with an audit trail if anyone later disputes it.

**First wedge:** Longevity and concierge clinics already trying to unify wearable + lab + notes data (Longevitix's customer base) who need physician liability cover before they'll rely on device data for a treatment decision — sell as the trust layer underneath their existing aggregation platform.

**What kills it:** Liability runs the wrong direction — if you're the one attesting the data was clean and something goes wrong, you're now the one who gets sued, and wearable makers may prefer to own that trust relationship directly with insurers rather than hand it to a middleman.

**Weirder variant:** Sell straight to life insurers as a real-time underwriting feed — healthy policyholders opt into continuous attested monitoring in exchange for dynamically-repriced premiums instead of an annual physical.

## Where I might be wrong

**WetLab Ledger is the weakest of the three.** The premise assumes a wet-lab capacity shortage severe enough to sustain a marketplace, but I didn't find hard evidence of that bottleneck being reported anywhere — it's inferred from funding velocity, not from labs themselves saying they're overwhelmed. It's also the idea most exposed to vertical integration by the very companies (Earendil, Iambic) whose funding I'm citing as the "why now," which could hollow out the addressable market before it forms. Worth a call to a mid-size CRO before spending more time here. ProtocolAudit's core assumption — that n=1 causal inference on slow-moving biomarkers can reach real statistical confidence — also needs a sanity check from an actual biostatistician; if it can't, the product is just confidence theater with extra steps.
