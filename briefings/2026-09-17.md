**The underexplored gap**
Entry-level hiring is broken in a specific, non-generic way: [88% of companies now run AI-based resume screening](https://builtin.com/articles/ai-entry-level-job-replacement), candidates are white-font-hacking their resumes to beat the parser, a single junior req pulls hundreds of applications within hours, and grad unemployment (22-27) has hit 5.8%. The Greenhouse CEO calls it a "mad arms race." What nobody's built: a way for a junior candidate to prove real competence that doesn't route through a resume parser at all — because the parser is the thing that's broken, not the candidate's ability to signal skill.

## Idea 1 — Groundtruth

**One-line pitch:** A tamper-evident work log that lets junior candidates skip the resume parser entirely and show verified proof of skill.

**The problem:** Junior job-seekers are stuck feeding a keyword-matching ATS that 88% of companies use, with hundreds of competing applicants per req and no way to signal real ability except by gaming the parser. The candidates who'd actually pass a work sample never get one — they get auto-rejected first.

**Why now:** Cheap, ubiquitous sandboxed dev/work environments (Replit-style, agent-native IDEs) now make it trivial to instrument a real task with a cryptographically signed activity log — timestamps, edit history, tool calls, no LLM-pasting shortcuts. That instrumentation layer didn't exist cheaply three years ago; now it's a commodity.

**Mechanism:** Candidates complete short, real tasks (a debugging session, a support ticket, a spreadsheet model) inside an instrumented sandbox that logs process, not just output, and hashes the session. Groundtruth issues a portable, verifiable "proof-of-work" score employers can check without re-running the task. It plugs into Greenhouse/Lever as a pre-screen that replaces keyword parsing for junior reqs.

**First wedge:** Laid-off or never-hired CS grads active in r/cscareerquestions and Blind — distribute as a free tool that gets you *past* the ATS, not a paid credential, then sell the employer-side verification API to mid-market companies drowning in junior applications.

**What kills it:** Employers don't actually want fewer resumes to read — they want plausible deniability for auto-rejecting most of them, and a tool that surfaces more qualified candidates just means more manual review work they don't want to do.

**Weirder variant:** Same tamper-evident session log, but for proving a freelance deliverable was done by a human and not an agent — a "proof-of-humanity" layer for marketplaces (Upwork-style) where clients are increasingly worried they're paying human rates for agent output.

## Idea 2 — Severance Ledger

**One-line pitch:** Turns a laid-off worker's actual work history into a skill graph matched against roles that need "AI-agent fluency," not credentials.

**The problem:** Someone just cut in the Meta/Intuit/Salesforce-style AI-driven layoffs has severance money and weeks to reskill, but generic reskilling platforms (Coursera-style courses) teach content, not the specific new hiring bar — "AI skills, adaptability, judgment" — that employers are now explicitly screening for. There's no product that reads what you *actually did* at your last job and tells you what's transferable to an agent-augmented role.

**Why now:** Brighteye's own thesis (per this week's fund close) is that the learning layer is moving into the workplace — and for the first time there's a large, funded, urgent cohort (severance-backed, time-boxed) motivated to reskill immediately rather than "someday." That specific moment — mass AI-cited layoffs plus severance runway — is new to 2026.

**Mechanism:** Ingest a user's real work exhaust (Jira history, Slack threads, code commits, ticket logs — with consent, via OAuth to their old employer's tools before access is revoked) to build a competency graph of what they actually did, not what their title claims. Match that graph against live job postings scored for "agent-fluency" requirements, and generate a 2-week micro-upskill path to close the specific gap for a specific req, not a generic course.

**First wedge:** Partner with outplacement firms and severance packages at companies mid-layoff (there's a fresh list every week) — they already pay for outplacement support and would rather pay for something that visibly works.

**What kills it:** Getting OAuth access to a former employer's tools during an active layoff is an IT and legal nightmare — most companies revoke access same-day, which cuts off the ledger's core data source right when it's most useful.

**Weirder variant**: Flip it — instead of reskilling the human, the laid-off worker sells their residual institutional knowledge *to* the AI agent that replaced them, via a paid knowledge-transfer interview the company buys as an offboarding product. The ledger becomes the training data pipeline for their own replacement.

## Idea 3 — Agent P&L

**One-line pitch:** An audit layer that measures whether AI-agent headcount cuts actually deliver ROI, before the next round of layoffs.

**The problem:** Gartner's own data says 80% of companies cutting headcount for AI agents see no ROI correlation — meaning most of these cuts are faith-based, not measured, and the CFOs approving them have no real instrumentation on whether the agent is actually doing the job at the quality/cost the human did. Boards are starting to ask, and no one has an answer.

**Why now:** Agents are now doing enough real production work (support tickets, code merges, onboarding tasks) to generate real telemetry — cost per resolution, error/escalation rate, cycle time — that wasn't available when "AI headcount reduction" was mostly a press-release claim rather than an operating reality.

**Mechanism:** Agent P&L ingests telemetry from the actual agent platforms a company uses (support, HR, dev-agent tooling) and produces a continuously updated cost/quality comparison against the human baseline it replaced, flagging when quality is silently degrading behind a lower headcount number. Sold as a dashboard to People Analytics and Finance, sitting between the AI vendor's own (self-interested) reporting and the board.

**First wedge:** Mid-market companies (500-5,000 employees) that already cut headcount citing AI and are now facing board pressure to prove it worked — sell into Finance/People Analytics as the tool that lets them get ahead of an uncomfortable audit.

**What kills it:** No company wants to formally instrument proof that its own headcount-reduction narrative was wrong — the buyer (the exec who made the cut) has every incentive to avoid measuring it, so the real buyer has to be the board or activist investor, which is a much harder, slower sale.

**Weirder variant:** Turn the same measurement engine outward as a public rating agency — a Glassdoor-meets-short-seller-report that scores public companies' AI-labor claims for investors, feeding activist funds looking for "AI-washing" the way ESG funds hunt greenwashing.

## Where I might be wrong

Severance Ledger is the weakest of the three — the data-access assumption (OAuth into a former employer's tools post-layoff) is close to a legal non-starter, and I'm not confident there's a workaround that preserves the "reads what you actually did" mechanism rather than degrading into another self-reported resume tool, which is the exact problem it's trying to avoid. Groundtruth's biggest unverified assumption is that employers actually want a higher-signal junior applicant pool — it's plausible the real bottleneck is reviewer capacity, not signal quality, which would make the whole wedge moot. Agent P&L has the clearest buyer logic but the slowest sales cycle (board/investor-level, not a single champion), so it may just take too long to get first revenue to validate before the next funding milestone.
