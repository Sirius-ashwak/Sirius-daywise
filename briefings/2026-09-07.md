## Three ideas

### 1. Ripcord
**Pitch:** Auto-transitions compounded-GLP-1 patients onto legal, covered alternatives before their supply gets cut off.

**The problem:** Millions of patients get compounded semaglutide/tirzepatide through telehealth. FDA enforcement and the Novo Nordisk v. Hims & Hers suit are actively shutting down that supply chain this quarter. Patients don't know their options — branded coupon programs, new oral GLP-1s, insurance prior-auth appeals, the shrinking list of pharmacies still operating legally — and a hard stop means fast weight regain and a customer who churns to whoever solves it first.

**Why now:** This isn't a slow trend, it's a dated enforcement event happening in 2026 specifically. That's rare — most healthtech problems don't come with a deadline.

**Mechanism:** Patient uploads their current script and dosage history. The tool cross-references insurance formulary, manufacturer patient-assistance programs, new oral approvals, and remaining legal compounding pharmacies, then auto-generates the specific prior-auth appeal or new referral needed to bridge supply with zero gap.

**First wedge:** White-label it inside 2–3 mid-size telehealth GLP-1 providers who are about to lose their own compounding pipeline and need to retain patients before they bounce to a competitor.

**What kills it:** If enforcement stalls or gets stayed in court (it's happened before with FDA actions like this), the urgency disappears and this is a vitamin, not a painkiller.

**Weirder variant:** Same "benefit/eligibility navigator" engine, sold as infrastructure to telehealth platforms for *any* drug class hitting a compounding crackdown or patent cliff — ketamine, testosterone, peptides.

---

### 2. Chaperone
**Pitch:** A runtime firewall that watches clinical AI agents' actions and blocks anything that leaks PHI or exceeds scope.

**The problem:** Hospitals are deploying ambient scribes and agentic EHR copilots faster than governance can track them. 88% of health IT leaders don't trust their infrastructure to contain the AI workloads already running, and untracked "shadow AI" on edge devices is creating PHI copies with no audit trail.

**Why now:** 2026 is the first year clinical AI is *agentic*, not just generative — it can write to the chart, place an order, or message a patient autonomously instead of drafting text for a human to review. That's a much bigger blast radius than anything existing HIPAA logging tools were built for.

**Mechanism:** A lightweight proxy sits between any clinical AI agent and the EHR/API surface it calls. Every tool call — read chart, write note, place order, send message — gets checked against a per-agent policy and logged in an immutable, plain-English-queryable audit trail.

**First wedge:** Sell to the AI vendors themselves (ambient scribe and copilot startups), not hospitals directly — it becomes the trust layer they point to in their own security questionnaires when *they're* selling into a health system.

**What kills it:** Epic or Oracle Health builds this natively into their own agent platforms and it becomes a checkbox feature instead of a company.

**Weirder variant:** Flip it to the patient side — a personal "AI power of attorney" that intercepts what any health AI (your insurer's, your hospital's, your own health app's) is allowed to do on your behalf, so individuals set their own consent policy instead of clicking through a 40-page ToS.

---

### 3. Ledger
**Pitch:** An AI that acts on your Whoop/Function Health/blood data instead of giving you one more dashboard to stare at.

**The problem:** Serious longevity users pay for 3–4 overlapping services — Whoop or Oura for daily biometrics, Function Health for quarterly bloodwork, a separate peptide or GLP-1 subscription — and none of it talks to each other. A resting-heart-rate spike sits in one app, a borderline lipid panel sits in another, and nobody connects them into an actual next step.

**Why now:** 2026 is the year consumer wearables cross into FDA-cleared clinical territory — Galaxy Ring's sleep apnea clearance, cuff-less BP coming to Galaxy Watch. For the first time, wearable data is precise enough to legally trigger a real clinical action, not just a wellness nudge.

**Mechanism:** Read-only integrations pull from existing wearable and lab APIs. A physician-authored rules engine runs over the combined stream, and when a threshold trips — say, a 3-week HRV decline plus rising ApoB — it doesn't render a chart, it drafts the next step (a lab order, a note to your prescriber, a protocol change) and routes it to a real physician for sign-off.

**First wedge:** Sell to concierge longevity clinics that already have physicians on staff but whose patients show up with five apps' worth of unread data — it becomes the clinic's differentiator, not a consumer app fighting Whoop for attention.

**What kills it:** Liability. The moment "AI drafts the next clinical step" touches an actual prescription, this is a med-device/telehealth regulatory problem, not a wellness app — and that changes the entire cost structure and timeline.

**Weirder variant:** Turn it inward on relationships instead of individuals — a household longevity ledger that pools a couple's or family's data and flags when one person's decline is being masked because they're being compared to a partner who's doing fine. No longevity app is built around the household instead of the individual.

## Where I might be wrong

**Ripcord is the weakest of the three.** It's a point-in-time regulatory arbitrage play, not obviously a durable company — if the FDA enforcement timeline slips (courts have stayed similar actions before), the urgency evaporates in a quarter. I also haven't verified how many patients are actually losing supply in the next 30–60 days versus this being a slower-moving story than the search summaries suggest, or whether the telehealth incumbents already have this transition flow built in-house, which would make it a feature, not a company. Chaperone's risk is the platform-absorption one (Epic ships this for free); Ledger's risk is that liability kills the wedge before it ever reaches a clinic. If I only chase one, verify Ripcord's actual patient-count/timeline first — that's the assumption everything else in that idea depends on.
