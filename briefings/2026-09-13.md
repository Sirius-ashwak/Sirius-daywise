Almost nobody's fixing the moment *before* any of that: **enterprise procurement of AI agents is running on the RFP templates built for static SaaS**, and they don't work — an agent's performance depends entirely on *your* data and edge cases, so a vendor demo on their sample data tells you nothing about how it'll behave on yours. ## Three ideas

### 1. Bakeoff
**One-line pitch:** A neutral clearinghouse that runs rival AI-agent vendors head-to-head on your own anonymized data before you sign.

**The problem:** Procurement leads at mid-market and enterprise companies (VP Ops, Head of CX, GC-level buyers) have to choose between 3-5 vendors selling functionally identical AI agents — a support agent, a contract-review agent, a claims agent — based on sales-deck demos run on the vendor's own curated data. There's no way to compare them on *your* messy tickets, your edge cases, your compliance rules, before the contract is signed. Whoever picks wrong eats a 6-12 month re-procurement cycle and looks bad doing it.

**Why now:** The sandboxing and synthetic-data tooling that let last week's "Gauntlet" idea stress-test one already-chosen agent has matured enough in 2026 to run the *same* harness against multiple competing vendors in parallel, fast — which wasn't computationally or economically viable even a year ago. At the same time, procurement teams have now lived through a year of failed pilots and are actively asking vendors "can we test this on our data first" — the demand already exists, nobody's built the neutral venue for it.

**Mechanism:** Buyer uploads a representative, PII-stripped slice of their real workload (tickets, contracts, claims — whatever the agent category is); Bakeoff runs the shortlisted vendors' agents against identical inputs in isolated sandboxes, scores them on accuracy, cost-per-resolution, latency, and policy violations, and hands the buyer a single comparison report instead of five sales decks. Vendors opt in because losing a bakeoff they didn't participate in looks worse than losing one they did.

**First wedge:** Target the systems integrators and procurement consultancies who already run vendor selection for mid-market companies buying their first CX or legal-ops agent — sell it as the evaluation layer they bolt onto an existing RFP process, not a new category they have to explain.

**What kills it:** Vendors collude to avoid participating, the way many refuse third-party benchmark leaderboards today — if the 3 biggest players in a category all decline, the bakeoff only ever includes challengers, and buyers stop trusting the results.

**Weirder variant:** Flip it from a one-time procurement tool into a standing subscription — Bakeoff keeps re-running the incumbent agent against challengers quarterly on fresh data, so buyers get an early warning when a competitor's agent has quietly overtaken the one they're paying for, before the renewal conversation even starts.

### 2. Stitchline
**One-line pitch:** An AI data layer that unifies a PE roll-up's acquired businesses without ripping out any of their ancient software.

**The problem:** PE-backed roll-ups of local service businesses — vet clinics, dental groups, HVAC shops, accounting practices — are one of 2026's biggest deployment stories for capital, and every acquisition drags in a decades-old practice-management or scheduling system with no API and a owner who panics at "we're migrating your software." The roll-up's central ops team ends up flying blind across 20-80 locations because standardizing on one system is a multi-year, trust-destroying project, but staying fragmented means no shared reporting, pricing, or scheduling intelligence.

**Why now:** Record PE dry powder is specifically chasing these fragmented, boring-but-cash-flowing verticals in 2026. What's new is that computer-use and agentic RPA finally got reliable enough this year to read and write against ancient, undocumented desktop software the way a human employee would — clicking through the same screens the front-desk staff use — which is the only way into systems that were never built to be integrated.

**Mechanism:** Deploys a lightweight agent at each acquired location that watches and operates its existing legacy system exactly as the local staff do — no rip-and-replace, no new front-end for employees to learn — while streaming a normalized version of that data (appointments, invoices, inventory, staffing) up to one dashboard for the roll-up's central team. Local businesses keep their comfort; corporate gets its first real cross-portfolio view.

**First wedge:** The messy v1 is one integration engineer at a single 15-clinic veterinary or dental roll-up, currently building this by hand with brittle screen-scraping scripts. Reach the next one through the PE operating partners and roll-up "platform CEOs" who are already living this pain and talk to each other constantly at industry conferences.

**What kills it:** Legacy vendors start locking down their UI or actively blocking automated access once they notice traffic that isn't a human clicking — a software-side arms race that a small startup can't win against an incumbent with a captive install base.

**Weirder variant:** Once the data layer exists across a portfolio, sell the aggregated, anonymized cross-location benchmarks back to the PE firm as pricing and staffing intelligence — "clinics like yours in this market charge 12% more for the same visit" — turning the integration plumbing into a pricing-strategy product.

### 3. Handoff
**One-line pitch:** Auto-generates the right human-approval screen for any AI agent action, sized to the actual risk.

**The problem:** Every enterprise team deploying agents — finance, legal, support, ops — is independently building bespoke "are you sure?" interfaces: a Slack button for one workflow, a custom dashboard for another, an email approval chain for a third. None of it scales as the agent fleet grows past a handful of use cases, and the interfaces don't reflect risk — a $50k wire transfer and a routine refund get the same flat "Approve/Reject" button, so people rubber-stamp both the same way.

**The specific problem, sharper:** The compliance and platform-engineering leads who own the "human-in-the-loop" requirement for regulated agent actions (finance ops, legal, healthcare) are the ones stuck reinventing this per agent, per team, with no shared standard — and audit teams increasingly want to see *why* an approval screen showed what it showed, not just that a button got clicked.

**Why now:** Generative-UI-for-agents is having a real design moment in 2026 (see Wavespace's framework this week) — the idea that an agent's interface should be built per-action rather than one static chat window is moving from concept to something teams are actively trying to build. Regulated industries are also starting to face explicit expectations that human-in-the-loop checkpoints be documented and auditable, not just present.

**Mechanism:** A single API any agent framework calls before taking a consequential action; Handoff classifies the action's risk and data type, then renders the appropriate interface on the fly — a big confirm dialog with the wire details laid out for a large transfer, an inline redline view for a contract clause, a map and ETA for a dispatch decision — and logs the full context shown to the human alongside their decision for audit.

**First wedge:** The messy v1 is a mid-size fintech or healthcare company's platform team currently maintaining three different Slack-bot approval flows by hand. Reach them through the same compliance-consulting and agent-implementation channels that Gauntlet and Bakeoff would use — this whole domain runs on the same few integrator relationships right now.

**What kills it:** This is the easiest of the three to swallow as a feature — every major agent framework (Bedrock Agents, LangGraph, the big platform vendors) has an obvious incentive to ship "smart approval UI" natively within a year, since they already own the orchestration layer this would sit on top of.

**Weirder variant:** Push the same risk-adaptive rendering downstream to the *customer* side, not just internal approvers — when an agent takes an action that affects an end customer (a subscription change, a claims decision), the customer gets the same tailored, context-rich confirmation screen instead of a generic email, turning the approval layer into a trust/UX product companies license for their own users.

## Where I might be wrong

**Handoff** is the weakest — I flagged its own failure mode honestly (it's a UI layer sitting on infrastructure someone else owns, and "smart approval screens" is a natural roadmap item for any agent orchestration platform, not a defensible standalone company). The assumption that needs testing: whether compliance teams actually want a *neutral, cross-vendor* approval layer for audit consistency, or whether they're fine with each agent platform shipping its own — if it's the latter, there's no wedge here at all. **Bakeoff** also leans on an assumption I haven't stress-tested: that vendors will voluntarily participate in a bakeoff they might lose. If the market leaders in any given agent category simply refuse (the way many AI labs dodge independent benchmarks today), the whole model degrades into "challenger vendors compete for scraps" — useful for buyers picking among underdogs, useless for the far more common case of choosing between two or three entrenched leaders.
