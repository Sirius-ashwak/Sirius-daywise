**Gap I'm seeing**
Note: my search did not surface an actual HN/X/Reddit thread, so this is inferred from the news above, not a cited discussion. The gap: everyone is racing to *supply* power (batteries, nuclear, gas), while the queue is really a *trust* problem. Utilities can't tell which large loads will actually curtail, and installers can't tell which houses can take a heat pump without a $4k panel upgrade. Both are "prove your load shape" problems, and nobody sells that proof.

## Idea 1 — Curtail Bond

**One-line pitch:** Third-party verified promise that a data center will cut load on demand, so it gets connected sooner.

**The specific problem:** Mid-size AI cloud and colo operators (20–150 MW) stuck in ERCOT/PJM large-load queues. Utilities are being told to make new loads bring their own power, but have no credible way to score a "we'll be flexible" promise. Operators say it; nobody believes it.

**Why now:** Texas paused new connections and PJM is shifting the burden onto large loads. Flexibility is now the currency for getting connected. Cheap batteries plus workload schedulers make curtailment technically real; what's missing is a verifier.

**Mechanism:** You install metering plus a control layer that ties the site's battery, backup gen, and training/inference job scheduler to a curtailment signal, run test events, and publish a signed, utility-readable curtailment record. A surety partner backs the promise: if the site fails to drop when called, the bond pays the utility. Revenue is a certification fee plus a cut of the premium.

**First wedge:** AI-cloud operators in ERCOT who lost their slot to the pause and need a re-application story. Reach them via the interconnection consultants and power brokers they already pay.

**What kills it:** Utilities and ISOs never formally credit third-party verification, so it stays a nice PDF. Also, the surety market may refuse to price tail risk on hyperscale-adjacent loads.

**Weirder variant:** Sell the same score to the *neighbors*. Residential ratepayers' groups and municipalities use it to opt into hosting only data centers with a verified bond, turning it into a local zoning tool.

## Idea 2 — Panel Proof

**One-line pitch:** Uses smart meter history to prove a house can take a heat pump without a panel upgrade.

**The specific problem:** Heat pump installers in California lose deals or eat costs when a panel upgrade is assumed necessary. Load calculations are done conservatively by hand, and permit packages differ by city. Acute for small installers doing 5–20 jobs a month.

**Why now:** SB 222 aims to clear permitting friction, heat pump volume keeps growing, and utilities already expose 15-minute interval data through Green Button. Calculating from actual metered peak instead of nameplate is allowed in code in many jurisdictions but rarely used.

**Mechanism:** Homeowner authorizes a meter-data pull, the tool computes measured peak load, adds the proposed heat pump/EV/battery, and generates the stamped-format load calculation and permit package for the specific city's checklist. It also flags when a small battery or load-limiting device (energy management system) closes the gap so the panel stays put.

**First wedge:** 10-person heat pump installers in the Bay Area and LA. Reach through distributor counters and the contractor Facebook and Slack groups; charge per permit package.

**What kills it:** Building departments reject meter-derived calcs, and each city needs bespoke handling. Also, utilities could throttle or change data access.

**Weirder variant:** Flip it to the seller side: real-estate listings show a "electrification headroom" score per house, so buyers know before closing which homes are cheap to convert.

## Idea 3 — Pad Finder

**One-line pitch:** Marketplace matching containerized battery operators with pre-screened parking lots that have grid headroom.

**The specific problem:** Branch-style operators need a parking-space-sized pad with the right interconnection headroom, a willing landlord, and quick permits. Site origination is manual and is now their growth ceiling as they scale. Commercial landlords with dead lots have no idea their pad has value.

**Why now:** Containerized "grid in a box" units install in two days, so the bottleneck moved from hardware to sites. Hosting-capacity maps are public in more states, and storage capital is abundant (Base $1B, SMT $268M).

**Mechanism:** Ingest utility hosting-capacity maps, parcel data, and zoning to score every commercial lot for storage-readiness. Landlords claim their lot, get an offer range, and operators bid. You take a finder fee plus a slice of the ground lease.

**First wedge:** Storage developers in Texas and Illinois (where Base already operates) who list site acquisition as their top constraint; landlords via commercial brokers and strip-mall REIT owners.

**What kills it:** Hosting-capacity maps are stale or wrong, so scores are unreliable. Big operators may build site-sourcing in-house, and a few landlords may hold out.

**Weirder variant:** Put the pads *at stranded substations* and sell the capacity back to data centers as "pre-approved bridge power" while they wait in the queue.

## Where I might be wrong

Curtail Bond is the weakest. It depends on utilities and ISOs formally crediting a third-party verifier, and I have no evidence they will; the surety piece is also a guess. Verify by asking two interconnection consultants whether any utility has accepted outside curtailment attestations. Panel Proof's key assumption is that AHJs accept meter-derived load calcs; check that with a handful of California building departments before writing any code. Pad Finder is the most conventional, but I'd want to confirm hosting-capacity data quality in Texas first.
